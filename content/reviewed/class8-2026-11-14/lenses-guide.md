# Lenses: predict the image before you look


A lens changes the path of light that passes through it. Start with its shape, then test what it does to a bundle of parallel light beams.

## Two shapes, two useful predictions

For the ordinary glass or plastic lenses used in air in this chapter, a convex lens is thicker in the middle than at its edges and brings parallel light beams together. A concave lens is thinner in the middle and thicker at its edges and spreads parallel beams apart.

Imagine three torch beams drawn as parallel lines on paper. After a convex lens, sketch them moving towards one another. After a concave lens, sketch them spreading out. This comparison is about light direction, not a claim that every image will look larger or smaller in the same way.

## A magnifying glass is one case, not a rule for every distance

Nearby small print can look upright and larger through a convex lens. Move the object farther away and the image can change orientation and size. So "convex means always bigger" is not a safe rule. An object viewed through a concave lens appears upright and smaller.

Before each observation, record your prediction. Afterward, write what changed: object distance, image size, image orientation, or more than one of these. The object itself has not grown or shrunk.

## Keep the observation safe

Never look at the Sun through a lens or aim concentrated sunlight at anyone's eyes or skin. Use a classroom torch demonstration supervised by a teacher instead of experimenting with concentrated sunlight.
