## 1. Map scales and equivalent ratios

### Watch: Scale drawings

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XUOPGqKGbjk)

### Understand it

A scale is a ratio between a drawing length and the corresponding real length. At 1 : 50,000, 1 cm represents 50,000 cm. For 3 cm, multiply to get 150,000 cm, then convert to 1.5 km. The video uses scale drawings; a map scale works the same way. Keep both units labelled and visible until the final conversion.

### Try it yourself

At 1:50,000 scale, what real distance does 3 cm represent?

**Check:** 150,000 cm = 1.5 km.

### Work through an example

A map uses 1 : 25,000, so 1 cm on it represents 25,000 cm on the ground. A straight segment of 6 cm represents 6 × 25,000 = 150,000 cm. Since 100,000 cm = 1 km, that is 1.5 km. Keep the units the same when using the ratio. The straight map distance is not automatically the length of a winding road.

### Try a different case

A straight map segment is 4 cm at 1 : 75,000. A learner reports 300,000 km. Find the distance in kilometres and explain the unit error.

**Reasoning:** 4 × 75,000 = 300,000 cm, not kilometres. Divide by 100,000 cm per km to get 3 km. The scale relates matching units; multiplying centimetres by the scale factor still gives centimetres. A road distance would need its route measured.

## 2. Ratios with three or more terms

### Watch: Ratio sharing the total - Corbettmaths

corbettmaths

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cflZnf9H5l4)

### Understand it

For three-part sharing, add every ratio term before finding one part. In 2 : 3 : 5 there are 10 parts, so sharing 120 gives 12 per part and shares of 24, 36 and 60. Multiply every term by the same scale factor and check that the shares add to the total.

### Try it yourself

Share 120 in the ratio 2:3:5.

**Check:** 24, 36 and 60.

### Work through an example

Three teams share 180 counters in the ratio 2 : 4 : 3. There are 2 + 4 + 3 = 9 equal parts, so one part is 180 ÷ 9 = 20 counters. Their shares are 40, 80 and 60. Check both conditions: 40 + 80 + 60 = 180, and dividing every share by 20 gives 2 : 4 : 3. Dividing by three teams would give equal shares, not this ratio.

### Try a different case

Share 84 counters in the ratio 1 : 2 : 4. A learner gives 28 to each group. Check the total and the ratio, then correct the shares.

**Reasoning:** 28 + 28 + 28 does equal 84, but 28 : 28 : 28 simplifies to 1 : 1 : 1, not 1 : 2 : 4. There are 7 parts, each worth 84 ÷ 7 = 12 counters. The required shares are 12, 24 and 48.

## 3. Pie charts and proportional angles

### Watch: Reading pie graphs (circle graphs)

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4JqH55rLGKY)

### Understand it

A whole pie chart is 360°. A sector's share of the total is its angle divided by 360°. To draw a sector for 25%, calculate 25/100 × 360° = 90°. The video focuses on reading pie charts, so practise the drawing direction yourself: pick a percentage, turn it into an angle, then check that the angle divided by 360° returns your fraction.

### Try it yourself

What sector angle represents 25% of a class?

**Check:** 90°.

### Work through an example

In a survey, each of 30 pupils chooses exactly one of three options. The counts are 12, 10 and 8. Their pie-chart angles are 12/30 × 360° = 144°, 10/30 × 360° = 120° and 8/30 × 360° = 96°. The angles add to 360°, just as the counts add to 30. An angle is not a percentage: 144° represents 144/360 = 40% of this whole.

### Try a different case

In a class of 40, exactly 14 pupils choose chess and everyone chooses one activity. What angle represents chess? Would it still be 14% in a class of 50 with 14 choosing chess?

**Reasoning:** For 40 pupils, the angle is 14/40 × 360° = 126°, representing 35%. With 14 out of 50, the share is 28% and the angle is 100.8°. Neither share is 14%: a count needs its total before it can become a percentage.

## 4. Direct and inverse proportion

### Watch: GCSE Maths | Direct and Inverse Proportion

BBC Bitesize - GCSE Revision Support

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tpx3izkCXsg)

### Understand it

In direct proportion, one quantity divided by the other stays constant. In inverse proportion, their product stays constant. For a fixed job, doubling equally productive workers can halve the time, but only if the work can be shared without new delays. List a small table of workers and days first, check that the product stays constant, and state that assumption before using the model.

### Try it yourself

If 4 equally productive workers need 6 days, how long would 8 need for the same work?

**Check:** 3 days, assuming the same productivity and no other constraints.

### Work through an example

At a fixed price of 12 rupees each, 3 notebooks cost 36 rupees and 6 cost 72 rupees: cost ÷ number stays 12. For a different model, 3 identical pumps fill a fixed tank in 8 hours, so the job takes 24 pump-hours. Six such pumps would take 24 ÷ 6 = 4 hours, not 16. That inverse model assumes equal steady rates, simultaneous working and no shared-flow limits.

### Try a different case

Five equally productive workers complete a fixed job in 12 days. Under an inverse-proportion model, how long would 3 workers take? What quantity stays fixed, and why is 12 × 3/5 not the right calculation?

**Reasoning:** The fixed work is 5 × 12 = 60 worker-days, so 3 workers need 60 ÷ 3 = 20 days. Fewer workers need more time in this model. 12 × 3/5 gives 7.2 days, incorrectly making fewer workers finish sooner. The prediction depends on equal productivity and work that can be shared without added delays.
