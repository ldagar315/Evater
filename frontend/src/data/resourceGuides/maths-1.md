## 1. Square numbers and visual patterns

### Watch: Describing numerical relationships with polynomial identities

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/embed/uJwq67y5wAY)

### Understand it

Draw a 3 by 3 square of tiles. To grow it into a 4 by 4 square, add 4 tiles along one edge and 3 along the other: 7 new tiles. The corner is counted once. For an n by n square, the next border adds (n + 1) + n = 2n + 1 tiles.

### Try it yourself

What is the difference between 13² and 12²?

**Check:** 25, because (12 + 1)² - 12² = 2 × 12 + 1.

### Work through an example

A square array grows from 20 by 20 tiles to 21 by 21. Add a row of 21 and a column of 20: 41 new tiles. Counting 21 + 21 counts the new corner twice. The identity (n + 1)² - n² = 2n + 1 explains the border for any whole-number side n, not only this drawing.

### Try a different case

A square array gains 57 tiles when its side grows by one tile. Find the original and new side lengths without trying every square. Then find how many tiles are added when that original side grows by two instead of one.

**Reasoning:** Solve 2n + 1 = 57 to get n = 28, so the sides are 28 and 29. Growing from 28 to 30 adds 57 + 59 = 116 tiles, or 30² - 28² = 116. It is not 2 × 57, because the second border is longer than the first.

## 2. Square roots and estimating roots

### Watch: Approximating square roots

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/embed/Lh7NMBPFVZw)

### Understand it

A square root tells you the nonnegative side length of a square of a given area. To estimate √70, compare 8² = 64 with 9² = 81: √70 is between 8 and 9. Squaring 8.3 and 8.4 narrows the bound to 8.3 < √70 < 8.4. In the video's final example, the corrected bound is 11 < √123 < 12, not 144.

### Try it yourself

Between which consecutive integers does √70 lie?

**Check:** 8 and 9, because 64 < 70 < 81.

### Work through an example

To bound √50, use 7² = 49 and 8² = 64, so 7 < √50 < 8. Since 7.0² = 49 and 7.1² = 50.41, the tighter bound is 7.0 < √50 < 7.1. An estimate is not an exact equality. The symbol √50 denotes the nonnegative square root; solving x² = 50 would also allow its negative opposite.

### Try a different case

A learner says √90 = 9.5 exactly because 90 lies between 81 and 100. Check 9.4² and 9.5² to give a tighter bound. Does finding the positive root list every solution of x² = 90?

**Reasoning:** 9.4² = 88.36 and 9.5² = 90.25, so 9.4 < √90 < 9.5. The proposed 9.5 is not exact because its square is not 90. The equation x² = 90 has two real solutions, √90 and -√90; the radical symbol alone names the nonnegative one.

## 3. Cubes and cube roots

### Watch: Dimensions of a cube from its volume

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/embed/GpOxsRF1Cjc)

### Understand it

A cube has three equal dimensions. For 216, factor it as 2³ × 3³. Take one factor from each triplet: ∛216 = 2 × 3 = 6. Check the result by multiplying 6 × 6 × 6. The video works through the related cube with volume 512 cm³.

### Try it yourself

Find ∛216 and explain the result.

**Check:** 6, because 6 × 6 × 6 = 216.

### Work through an example

A cube with side 4 cm has volume 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 cm³. Doubling the side to 8 cm multiplies all three dimensions by 2, so the volume becomes 512 cm³, eight times as large, not twice. Taking a cube root reverses this volume calculation: ∛512 = 8. Keep cubic units for volume and length units for the side.

### Try a different case

A cube has volume 343 cm³. Find its side length. A second cube has eight times that volume. Is its side eight times as long? Calculate its volume and side to check.

**Reasoning:** 343 = 7 × 7 × 7, so the first side is 7 cm. The second volume is 8 × 343 = 2744 cm³ and its side is 14 cm, since 14³ = 2744. Multiplying volume by eight doubles the side; multiplying the side by eight would multiply volume by 8³ = 512.

## 4. Recognising perfect squares and cubes

### Watch: Simplifying square roots

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cw3mp8oNASk)

### Understand it

Prime factors help you recognise perfect powers. A perfect square has complete pairs of prime factors; a perfect cube has complete triplets. In 72 = 2³ × 3², one 2 is left unpaired, so it is not a perfect square. The video shows the pair-grouping idea with square roots; apply triplets separately for cubes.

### Try it yourself

Is 72 a perfect square?

**Check:** No. Its prime factors are 2³ × 3², so one factor 2 remains unpaired.

### Work through an example

For 144 = 2⁴ × 3², all prime-factor exponents are even, so it is a perfect square: 12². It is not a perfect cube because the exponents are not all multiples of three. For 216 = 2³ × 3³ the reverse holds: it is 6³ but not a perfect square. Test pairs and triplets separately; one perfect-power label does not imply the other.

### Try a different case

Find the smallest positive integer multiplier that makes 72 a perfect square, and separately the smallest that makes it a perfect cube. Check the resulting powers. Must the same multiplier work for both?

**Reasoning:** 72 = 2³ × 3². For a square, multiply by 2 to obtain 2⁴ × 3² = 144 = 12². For a cube, multiply by 3 to obtain 2³ × 3³ = 216 = 6³. Each multiplier supplies the missing factors for its own grouping rule. The smallest multipliers differ; making a square is not the same task as making a cube.
