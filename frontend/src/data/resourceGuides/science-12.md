## 1. Habitats and ecosystem components

### Watch: What are ecosystems?

KLU App

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P1X-WpfUvm4)

### Understand it

An ecosystem includes a community of living organisms and the non-living conditions with which they interact. A pond plant uses light and water; fish obtain dissolved oxygen from the water. The plant and fish are biotic components. Light, water and temperature are abiotic conditions. Soil is often listed as an abiotic component, but real soil can also contain living roots and microbes: name the particular component you mean.

A list is only the start of an explanation. Link a condition to an organism and say how it matters. Less water may change the space available to pond organisms; a temperature change can affect living processes. For a habitat visit, stay with your teacher and observe from a safe place without entering water or disturbing organisms.

### Try it yourself

In a school garden, a seedling grows in moist soil beside a stone. Identify two living components and two non-living components if a worm is also present. Then explain one interaction that a list alone would miss. Is every part of the soil sample necessarily non-living?

**Check:** The seedling and worm are living components; water in the soil and the stone are non-living components. The seedling takes up water through its roots, for example. A soil sample may include living organisms as well as non-living mineral particles and water, so classify the specified part rather than calling everything in the sample non-living.

## 2. Food chains and food webs

### Watch: Food Chains & Food Webs

FuseSchool - Global Education

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2lqhJNgn_Wg)

### Understand it

A food chain shows one feeding route; a food web connects several routes. For arrows showing the transfer of food and its energy, point from the organism eaten to the organism eating it. Grass → grasshopper → frog means the grasshopper eats grass and the frog eats the grasshopper. It does not mean that grass eats a grasshopper.

Suppose a garden bird eats both seeds and caterpillars. Seeds → bird and leaves → caterpillar → bird are two routes to the same consumer. Removing caterpillars removes one food source, not necessarily every food source. A web is a model of stated feeding links, not a guarantee of exact population changes.

### Try it yourself

Use only these feeding facts: snails eat lettuce, thrushes eat snails, and thrushes also eat fallen berries. Write two food chains ending with a thrush. If snails become scarce, which route remains? Does that prove the number of thrushes will stay unchanged?

**Check:** Lettuce → snail → thrush and berries → thrush. The berry route remains, but its presence does not prove there is enough food or that the thrush population stays unchanged. Berry supply and other conditions also matter. The arrows point from food to eater.

## 3. Decomposers and nutrient cycling

### Watch: How dirt works

The Nature Conservancy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K7YpPgbmSew)

### Understand it

Decomposers such as many fungi and bacteria break down dead organic material into simpler substances. Nutrients can return to soil or water and later be taken up by plants. Decomposition does not turn every kind of waste into plant nutrients at the same speed; plastic litter is not equivalent to a fallen leaf.

Separate the material pathway from the energy pathway. Materials from dead leaves can enter the soil and later new plants. In contrast, energy flows through organisms, with some transferred to the surroundings as heat. Decomposers also use energy; they do not send all that energy back to plants. In a sunlit garden, plants still need an input of light energy. Do not handle mould or unknown decaying material to investigate this.

### Try it yourself

A student says, 'When a fallen leaf decomposes, both its nutrients and all its energy return to a new plant, so the plant no longer needs sunlight.' Identify the two different pathways and correct the claim. Would putting plastic beside the leaf make both disappear at the same rate?

**Check:** Nutrients can be released and taken up again, so materials can cycle. Energy flows through the system and some spreads to the surroundings as heat; it is not all recycled to the plant. The garden plant still needs light for photosynthesis. Plastic and a leaf are different materials, so their breakdown is not expected to happen at the same rate.

## 4. Interdependence and ecosystem change

### Watch: Why is biodiversity so important? - Kim Preshoff

Kim Preshoff

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GK_vRtHJZu4)

### Understand it

Organisms depend on other organisms and on their surroundings. In a simple model, leaves feed caterpillars and caterpillars feed insect-eating birds. Removing many caterpillars can reduce feeding damage to leaves while also removing food for those birds. One change can have effects in different directions along different links.

Treat these as predictions to test, not promises. Birds may have other food, and weather or disease may also affect the populations. Compare observations over time and, where possible, with a similar place without the change. A fall in two populations at the same time is not enough by itself to show which caused which.

### Try it yourself

Imagine a pond model where small fish eat insect larvae and herons eat those fish. Only the small-fish population falls at first. Predict one possible change in larvae and one possible effect on herons, using the feeding links. Why can you not give exact new population numbers from this web alone?

**Check:** Larvae may increase because fewer small fish eat them. Herons may have less of that fish food available, even though they do not eat the larvae directly in this model. Other food, movement, conditions and rates of change are not given, so neither exact counts nor a certain heron decline follow from the web alone.
