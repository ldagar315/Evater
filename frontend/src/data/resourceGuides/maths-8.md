## 1. Fractions, decimals and percentages

### Watch: Fraction, decimal, and percent from visual model

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwg052FC_Zw)

### Understand it

A fraction, decimal and percentage can name the same share of a whole. For 7/20, divide 7 by 20 to get 0.35, or make an equivalent fraction 35/100. Both give 35%. Multiplying the decimal by 100 changes the notation to percent; it does not change the represented share.

Use a benchmark to catch place-value errors: 7/20 is more than 1/4 but less than 1/2, so its percentage should lie between 25% and 50%. The denominator tells you how the whole is divided. Seven shaded parts out of twenty equal parts is not 7% and not 35 whole objects.

### Try it yourself

Write 11/40 as a decimal and percentage. Give the unshaded share in all three forms if 11 of 40 equal parts are shaded. Use one-quarter as a check on the shaded answer.

**Check:** 11/40 = 0.275 = 27.5%, just above 1/4 = 25%. The unshaded share is 29/40 = 0.725 = 72.5%. The shares add to 1, the decimals to 1.000 and the percentages to 100%; 27.5% is not 0.275%.

## 2. Finding a percentage of a quantity

### Watch: Percentages without a calculator - Corbettmaths

corbettmaths

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9QKc5bZPLv0)

### Understand it

A percentage is a fraction of a named whole. Seventeen and a half percent of 240 can be built from 10%, 5% and 2.5%: 24 + 12 + 6 = 42. Equivalently, 17.5/100 × 240 = 42. Write the whole and its unit before calculating. If the whole is a mass, the answer is a mass, not another percentage.

A larger percentage does not always give a larger amount when the wholes differ. You must calculate each share using its own whole. As a reverse check, dividing the amount by the decimal percentage should recover that whole.

### Try it yourself

Bag A has mass 360 g, of which 12.5% is red counters. Bag B has mass 200 g, of which 20% is red counters. Which bag has the greater mass of red counters, and by how much? Explain why comparing only 12.5 and 20 would fail.

**Check:** Bag A has 1/8 × 360 = 45 g of red counters; bag B has 1/5 × 200 = 40 g. A has 5 g more despite its lower percentage, because its whole is larger. The reverse check 45 ÷ 0.125 = 360 recovers A's whole mass.

## 3. Percentage increase and decrease

### Watch: Percentage Change - Corbettmaths

corbettmaths

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q2gRAS08fE0)

### Understand it

Percentage change compares the change with the original amount: change/original × 100%. A rise from ₹160 to ₹200 adds ₹40, which is 25% of ₹160. Returning from ₹200 to ₹160 removes the same ₹40, but the downward change starts from the original ₹200, so the decrease is 20%. Equal rupee changes need not be equal percentage changes.

For repeated changes, apply each percentage to the amount at that step. A 20% rise multiplies by 1.20; a 20% fall multiplies by 0.80. Their product is 0.96, not 1.00. Writing each new base prevents the tempting but incorrect cancellation of equal-looking percentages.

### Try it yourself

A hypothetical price of ₹500 rises by 20%, then the new price falls by 20%. Find both new prices and the net percentage change from ₹500. What percentage reduction from the intermediate price would return exactly to ₹500?

**Check:** The rise gives ₹600; 20% of ₹600 is ₹120, so the fall gives ₹480. The net change is a ₹20 decrease, or 4% of the original ₹500. To return from ₹600 to ₹500, reduce by ₹100/₹600 × 100% = 16⅔%, not 20%. The two changes use different bases.

## 4. Applying percentages in context

### Watch: Cost price, selling price, profit and loss

Khan Academy India - English

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aB_aXOWI_FI)

Compare selling price with cost price in the video. It is a basic profit, loss and break-even primer, not a lesson on discount percentages or profit margins. For the original question below, use marked price as the base for a discount and cost price as the base for profit on cost. Name the whole before calculating, and account for other costs and taxes only when the question includes them.

### Understand it

A shop buys a bag for ₹720, marks it ₹1,000 and gives a 10% discount on the marked price. The discount is ₹100, so the selling price is ₹900. The profit is ₹900 - ₹720 = ₹180. For profit as a percentage of cost, use ₹720 as the whole: 180/720 × 100% = 25%. A discount can therefore coexist with a profit: the two percentages use different bases.

Write the base beside each calculation. Discount uses marked price; profit on cost uses cost price. Profit margin on sales is a different measure, so do not silently swap the denominator. These examples ignore other costs and taxes. If extra charges are stated, include them as directed rather than assuming this is a final checkout total.

### Try it yourself

A shop buys a lamp for ₹640 and marks it ₹1,000. It offers a 20% discount. Find the selling price, profit and profit percentage on cost. Explain why a 20% discount is not a 20% loss.

**Check:** Selling price ₹800; profit ₹160; profit on cost 160/640 × 100% = 25%. The discount is measured from ₹1,000, while profit or loss on cost is measured from ₹640.
