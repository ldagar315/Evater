## 1. Right triangles and the hypotenuse

### Watch: Pythagorean theorem 1

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JVrkLIcA2qw)

### Understand it

Mark the right angle before naming a side. In triangle ABC with a right angle at B, AB and BC meet at that angle, so they are the legs. AC is opposite B and is the hypotenuse. Rotating the page changes none of these roles. With AB = 9 cm and BC = 12 cm, AC² = 9² + 12² = 225, giving AC = 15 cm.

The letters a, b and c are placeholders, not fixed positions on a drawing. Write the hypotenuse squared alone, then check that your answer is longer than either leg. The theorem applies because a right angle is given, not because a sketch looks nearly square.

### Try it yourself

Triangle RST is right-angled at S. RS = 7 cm and ST = 24 cm. Name the hypotenuse and find its length. A learner writes RS² = ST² + RT² because RS looks horizontal. Explain the error.

**Check:** RT is opposite the right angle at S, so RT² = 7² + 24² = 625 and RT = 25 cm. RS is a leg, not the hypotenuse. Horizontal placement does not decide a side's role; 25 cm is longer than both 7 cm and 24 cm.

## 2. Finding an unknown side

### Watch: Pythagorean theorem 1

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JVrkLIcA2qw)

### Understand it

Choose addition or subtraction from the unknown side. If the legs are known, add their squares to find the hypotenuse squared. If the hypotenuse and one leg are known, subtract that leg's square. A right triangle with hypotenuse 17 cm and leg 8 cm has other leg x satisfying x² + 64 = 289. Thus x² = 225 and x = 15 cm.

Take the positive square root because a side length is positive. Substitute back into the original equation: 8² + 15² = 17². Adding instead of subtracting would produce a supposed leg longer than the hypotenuse, which cannot be right.

### Try it yourself

A right triangle has hypotenuse 37 cm and a leg of 12 cm. Find the other leg and check it. Separately, a right triangle has legs 20 cm and 21 cm. Find its hypotenuse. Why do these two questions need different operations?

**Check:** The missing leg is √(37² - 12²) = √1225 = 35 cm; 12² + 35² = 1369 = 37². For the second triangle, the hypotenuse is √(20² + 21²) = √841 = 29 cm. Subtract to isolate an unknown leg; add when the unknown is the hypotenuse.

## 3. Converse and Pythagorean triples

### Watch: Checking whether a triangle is right-angled

corbettmaths

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UKaoHO4ClS4)

### Understand it

The converse lets us decide whether three lengths form a right triangle. For positive lengths, first check that the two smaller lengths add to more than the largest. Then compare the largest square with the sum of the other two squares. For 9, 12 and 15, we have 9 + 12 > 15 and 81 + 144 = 225, so these lengths form a right triangle. For 9, 12 and 16, a triangle is possible but 81 + 144 ≠ 256, so it is not right-angled.

A Pythagorean triple has positive integer entries satisfying the equation. Scaling every side of a triple by the same positive integer keeps it a triple: both sides of the equation gain the same squared scale factor.

### Try it yourself

Classify 10, 24, 26; 10, 24, 25; and 4, 7, 12 as right triangle, non-right triangle or impossible triangle. Explain each decision rather than only naming a familiar triple.

**Check:** 10 + 24 > 26 and 10² + 24² = 676 = 26², so the first is right. The second forms a triangle but 676 ≠ 625, so it is non-right. For the last, 4 + 7 = 11 < 12, so no triangle exists. The first triple is twice 5, 12, 13; its squares are four times those of the smaller triple.

## 4. Area rearrangements and proof

### Watch: How many ways are there to prove the Pythagorean theorem? - Betty Fei

TED-Ed; educator Betty Fei

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YompsDlEdtc)

### Understand it

Place four congruent right triangles, each with legs a and b, inside a square of side a + b, leaving a central square. Each central side is a hypotenuse c. At a central corner, the neighbouring acute angles add to 90°, so the remaining angle is 180° - 90° = 90°. Equal sides and right angles justify calling the leftover shape a square.

The outer area is (a + b)² and the four triangle areas total 4 × ½ab = 2ab. Therefore c² = (a + b)² - 2ab = a² + b². For a = 6 and b = 8, the calculation is 196 - 96 = 100. The algebra, with arbitrary positive leg lengths, is what goes beyond checking that single example.

### Try it yourself

Use this rearrangement for legs 5 cm and 12 cm. Find the outer square's area, the four triangles' total area and the central square's side. Which part of the argument shows the result is general, rather than true only for these numbers?

**Check:** The outer area is 17² = 289 cm²; the four triangles total 4 × ½ × 5 × 12 = 120 cm². The central square has area 169 cm² and side 13 cm. The arbitrary-letter identity (a + b)² - 2ab = a² + b², together with the justified square construction, proves the relationship for every right triangle, not just this example.

## 5. The diagonal of a square and an isosceles right triangle

### Watch: The diagonal of a square

Math Mammoth

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Eo04qjLRiSY)

### Understand it

Draw a square of side 12 cm and join the midpoints of neighbouring sides. The tilted square inside leaves four equal corner triangles, each with perpendicular legs 6 cm and 6 cm. Each triangle has area ½ × 6 × 6 = 18 cm², so the tilted square has area 144 - 4 × 18 = 72 cm². Its side c is also the diagonal of a 6 cm square. Since c² = 72 and a length is positive, c = √72 = 6√2 cm, not 12 cm. The square built on this diagonal has twice the original 6 cm square's area: 72 cm² rather than 36 cm². This area picture builds the result before using the general theorem.

For any square of side s, the diagonal d satisfies d² = s² + s² = 2s². The factor of two relies on the two perpendicular sides being equal. For a rectangle with unequal sides a and b, the diagonal instead gives d² = a² + b². Do not replace that sum with twice the rectangle's area, 2ab, unless the sides really are equal.

### Try it yourself

A learner applies the square rule to a 9 cm by 12 cm rectangle and says that a square built on its diagonal must have area 2 × 9 × 12 = 216 cm². Check the claim by finding the rectangle's diagonal and the area of the square built on it. Which condition of the square argument was lost?

**Check:** The rectangle's diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle: d² = 9² + 12² = 81 + 144 = 225, so d = 15 cm. A square built on it has area 225 cm², not 216 cm². The rectangle itself has area 108 cm². Its unequal perpendicular sides do not supply two equal squares; the doubling rule belonged to an original square, not to every rectangle.

## 6. A history extension: what changes with cubes?

### Watch: From square-number triples to Fermat's question

University of Oxford

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1BSFyEIY2BY)

Use this brief historical overview to see how changing the exponent leads from Pythagorean triples to a different question. The theorem here concerns positive integers x, y and z and an integer exponent n greater than 2. Zero and non-integer values are outside those conditions. This is an overview, not a proof of the theorem. Checking one triple, or many triples, cannot replace a general proof. Keep the square and cube calculations below separate.

### Understand it

The power 2 matters. Although 3² + 4² = 5², replacing every square with a cube does not keep the equality: 3³ + 4³ = 27 + 64 = 91, while 5³ = 125. One familiar right-triangle triple cannot simply be reused for a different power.

Fermat's Last Theorem says that xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ has no solution in positive integers x, y and z when n is an integer greater than 2. It was a long-standing open problem in history, but Andrew Wiles proved it in the 1990s. You are not expected to prove it here. The conditions matter: a positive integer is a whole number greater than zero, and n = 2 is not covered. One failed cube example does not prove a claim about all triples.

### Try it yourself

Two learners propose counterexamples for n = 3. One sets x = 0, y = 4 and z = 4. The other sets x = 1, y = 2 and z equal to the positive cube root of 9. Check whether each equation balances, then decide whether either disproves the stated theorem. Check the allowed numbers, not just the arithmetic.

**Check:** The first equation balances: 0³ + 4³ = 4³. But zero is not positive, so that triple is outside the theorem's conditions. The second also balances because 1³ + 2³ = 9 and z³ = 9. However, 2³ < 9 < 3³, so this z lies between 2 and 3 and is not an integer. Neither is a counterexample to the positive-integer claim. Verifying the arithmetic is necessary, but a proposed counterexample must also satisfy every condition.
