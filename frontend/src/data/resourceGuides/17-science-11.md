## 1. Rotation, revolution and time

### Think it through

Earth's rotation is its spin and gives the day-night cycle. Revolution is its motion around the Sun and is linked to the year. Draw separate arrows for those motions. A month is linked to the Moon's cycle; do not use one motion to explain all three time intervals.

### Try it yourself

Which motion gives Earth its day-night cycle?

**Check:** Earth's rotation.

## 2. Moon phases

### Watch: What causes the phases of the moon?

### Understand it

The Sun lights half the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of that sunlit half, giving the ordinary phases. A crescent is not normally Earth's shadow falling on the Moon. Draw the Sun, Earth and Moon, then mark the illuminated half before shading the view from Earth.

### Try it yourself

Is a crescent Moon normally a partial lunar eclipse?

**Check:** No. Phases are the changing view of the Moon's sunlit half.

## 3. Lunar and solar calendars

### Think it through

A lunar month follows the Moon's phase cycle, while a solar year follows Earth's annual cycle around the Sun. Twelve lunar months are shorter than a solar year. Without adjustments, a calendar based only on those months shifts relative to the seasons. Ask what each calendar is trying to keep aligned.

### Try it yourself

Why can a calendar based only on lunar months drift relative to the seasons?

**Check:** Twelve lunar months are shorter than a solar year.

## 4. Leap years and calendar adjustment

### Think it through

Calendar adjustments handle the mismatch between whole days and the length of a year. In the Gregorian calendar, years divisible by 4 are usually leap years, but century years must also be divisible by 400. Thus 2000 was a leap year and 2100 will not be. Name the calendar before applying its rule.

### Try it yourself

In the Gregorian calendar, is 2100 a leap year?

**Check:** No. Century years must be divisible by 400 to be leap years.
