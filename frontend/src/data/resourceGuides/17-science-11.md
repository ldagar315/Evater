## 1. Rotation, revolution and time

### Watch: Earth's rotation and revolution

Crash Course Kids

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l64YwNl1wr0)

### Understand it

Rotation is an object's spin about its own axis; revolution is its motion around another body. Earth's rotation is the main cause of the daily apparent motion of the Sun and the day-night cycle. Earth's revolution around the Sun is linked to the year. Do not use one orbit to explain every time interval.

A model needs two separate motions. Mark a town on a globe and turn the globe in front of a fixed lamp: the mark moves into and out of the lit half. Moving the globe around the lamp represents a different motion. The Moon's repeating phases provide another cycle, about 29.5 days, rather than a fixed 30-day month. Use a classroom model, never look directly at the Sun.

### Try it yourself

A learner labels Earth's spin 'one year' and its trip around the Sun 'one day'. Correct the two labels. Which separate observed cycle suggests a lunar month? Would counting exactly 30 days always return to precisely the same Moon phase?

**Check:** Earth's spin is linked to the day-night cycle; its revolution around the Sun is linked to the year. The repeating cycle of Moon phases suggests a lunar month. Its average length is about 29.5 days, so exactly 30 days is not always precisely one phase cycle. These are different cycles, not three names for Earth's rotation.

## 2. Moon phases

### Watch: What causes the phases of the moon?

Veritasium

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jip3BbZBpsM)

### Understand it

The Sun lights approximately half the Moon at a time. As the Moon moves around Earth, we see different fractions of that sunlit half. Near full Moon we see nearly all the illuminated half; near new Moon most of the hemisphere facing us is unlit. At a quarter phase, the visible disc looks half lit. The word 'quarter' refers to the stage in the phase cycle, not a quarter-lit visible disc.

Ordinary phases are not caused by Earth's shadow moving over the Moon. Earth's shadow causes a lunar eclipse when the alignment is right. A lamp and ball model can help: keep the lamp in one place and identify which half of the ball it lights before deciding which part an observer sees. Never look directly at the Sun or try to observe it through binoculars or a telescope.

### Try it yourself

A student draws a half-lit Moon and says, 'Earth must be covering the other half with its shadow, and the Sun is lighting only a quarter of the Moon.' Correct both claims. In an ordinary crescent phase, has most of the Moon stopped receiving sunlight?

**Check:** A half-lit visible disc can be a quarter phase: the Sun still lights about half the whole Moon, but we see only part of that lit half. Earth's shadow is not the ordinary phase explanation. During a crescent, about half the Moon is still sunlit; only a thin part of that sunlit half is visible from Earth. An eclipse is a different alignment event.

## 3. Lunar and solar calendars

### Think it through

A lunar month follows the Moon's phase cycle; a solar year follows the annual seasonal cycle. Using rounded values, 12 × 29.5 = 354 days, about 11 days shorter than a 365-day solar year. A calendar made only from 12 lunar months therefore begins its next year earlier relative to the seasons. This difference accumulates if there is no adjustment.

A purely lunar calendar can follow Moon phases without staying aligned to seasons. A solar calendar adjusts whole days to stay close to the solar year. Some luni-solar calendars add an extra month in selected years to keep both cycles approximately aligned. Their rules differ, so an approximate difference is not a rule for predicting an actual festival date.

### Try it yourself

Use the rounded model of a 29.5-day lunar month and a 365-day solar year. How long are 24 lunar months? How far short is that of two solar years? Would adding one day to every second lunar year remove the whole difference? Explain why some calendars need a larger adjustment.

**Check:** 24 × 29.5 = 708 days, while 2 × 365 = 730 days: a difference of 22 days in this rounded model. Adding one day would still leave 21 days, so it would not remove the difference. Selected extra months can help a luni-solar calendar keep in step with seasons, but its actual rules must be checked separately. These rounded numbers are not an exact calendar-date prediction.

## 4. Leap years and calendar adjustment

### Think it through

A calendar has to use whole days, while the seasonal year is not exactly 365 days. Adding a leap day roughly every four years helps, but that alone adds too many days over long periods. The Gregorian rule corrects this with century exceptions.

Use this decision chain for a Gregorian year: if it is not divisible by 4, it is not a leap year. If it is divisible by 4 but not by 100, it is a leap year. If it is divisible by 100, it is a leap year only if it is also divisible by 400. For example, 2028 passes the ordinary four-year rule; 1900 fails the century exception; 2000 passes the 400-year exception. A Gregorian leap year has 366 days, including 29 February. Other calendars need their own rules.

### Try it yourself

A student says 2100 and 2400 must both be Gregorian leap years because both divide by 4. Check that claim, then decide whether 2027 and 2032 are leap years. Explain which test settles each case.

**Check:** 2100 is not a leap year: divisible by 100 but not 400. 2400 is a leap year: divisible by 400. 2027 is not divisible by 4, so is not a leap year. 2032 is divisible by 4 but not 100, so is a leap year.
