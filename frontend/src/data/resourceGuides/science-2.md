## 1. Cells and the microscope

### Watch: Microscopes and How to Use a Light Microscope

Amoeba Sisters

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tVcEEw6qbBQ)

### Understand it

A microscope reveals structures too small to see clearly with the naked eye. Magnification enlarges an image; resolution determines whether nearby details can be distinguished. Enlarging a blurred image alone does not reveal detail that was never resolved. Watch the video's slide preparation only as a demonstration, and use teacher-prepared slides under a teacher's guidance.

A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of living organisms. Typical plant and animal cells have a membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus. Typical plant cells also have a cell wall; green plant cells contain chloroplasts, but not every plant cell is green. Animal cells do not have a cell wall or chloroplasts. Bacterial cells differ: they do not have a well-defined nucleus. A labelled diagram is a simplified model, not a photograph of every cell.

### Try it yourself

A teacher's cell diagram has a membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus and cell wall, but no chloroplasts are drawn. A learner calls it an animal cell just because it is not green. Is that conclusion justified? Separately, would enlarging a blurry microscope photograph four times guarantee four times as much structural detail?

**Check:** No. A cell wall is consistent with a plant cell, and plant cells need not all contain chloroplasts. Missing a drawn feature is not by itself a full identification of an organism. Enlarging the photograph changes image size, not necessarily resolution, so it cannot guarantee new structural detail. Use teacher-prepared slides only; do not prepare body samples yourself.

## 2. Levels of organisation

### Watch: Biological Levels in Biology: The World Tour

Amoeba Sisters

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EtWknf1gzKo)

### Understand it

In a multicellular organism, cells can work together in tissues; different tissues work together in organs; organs cooperate in organ systems. The organism is the complete living individual. At each level, explain how the parts cooperate rather than only reciting the names. Focus on the video from cells to organism; its later ecosystem levels are an extension.

An organ is not simply a very large cell, and a single tissue is not a whole organ. The full hierarchy describes organisation in complex multicellular bodies. A unicellular organism carries out its life processes within one cell, so it need not have separate tissues and organs.

### Try it yourself

Order these examples from the smaller level to the whole individual: human body, nervous system, nerve cell, brain, nervous tissue. Explain why the brain is not just one tissue. A learner says a single-celled organism must contain miniature organs to be alive. What is wrong with that claim?

**Check:** The order is nerve cell, nervous tissue, brain, nervous system, human body. The brain is an organ containing different tissues working together. A single-celled organism carries out necessary life processes within its one cell; it does not need the same tissue-organ hierarchy as a human. Keep the kind of organism in view when applying the model.

## 3. Kinds of microorganisms

### Think it through

Microorganisms include bacteria, many fungi, protozoa and microscopic algae. Some are unicellular, and some have many cells. Size alone does not put them all in the same group. Yeast is a unicellular fungus, whereas a bacterium belongs to a different group even though it is also one cell. Treat viruses separately when considering their dependence on host cells.

Compare a named organism and its role, not the slogan 'all microbes are germs'. Some microbes help produce food or recycle materials; others can cause disease or spoil food. A useful role for one example does not make every unknown sample safe to handle, smell or eat.

### Try it yourself

A learner makes two rules: 'Every fungus has many cells' and 'Every microorganism harms us'. Use yeast in bread-making and bacteria involved in curd formation to assess the rules. Another learner says an unknown cottony growth on food must be safe because some microbes make food. Is that a valid conclusion?

**Check:** Yeast is a single-celled fungus, so the first rule fails. Yeast and the bacteria used in curd formation provide useful examples, so the second rule also fails. None of that identifies an unknown growth as safe. Do not handle, sniff, taste or culture unknown microbial growth; learn from prepared materials and teacher-provided observations.

## 4. Useful microbes and safe practice

### Watch: The beneficial bacteria that make delicious food - Erez Garty

Erez Garty

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eksagPy5tmQ)

### Understand it

Microbes can change food through processes such as fermentation. Yeast releases carbon dioxide while breaking down food, and gas bubbles help dough rise. Bacteria involved in curd formation produce lactic acid; do not assume every useful food microbe makes exactly the same product. Connect the organism, the process and the observed change.

Use teacher-provided observations for comparisons. If two mixtures differ in temperature, composition and yeast presence, one result cannot isolate which difference mattered. The video gives useful food examples, not a test of whether an unknown mixture is edible. Do not culture unknown microbes or taste experimental mixtures at home.

### Try it yourself

In a fictional teacher record, two otherwise comparable dough samples begin at 20 mm height. With yeast, the final height is 35 mm; without added yeast, it is 21 mm. Compare the increases. How does yeast's carbon dioxide provide an explanation, and does the height record alone chemically identify the gas or prove the mixture is safe to eat?

**Check:** The increases are 15 mm and 1 mm, so the yeast sample rises 14 mm more in this record. Carbon dioxide released by yeast can form bubbles that expand dough. Height data is consistent with this explanation but does not itself chemically identify a gas or establish food safety. Do not taste experimental mixtures or culture unknown microbes.
