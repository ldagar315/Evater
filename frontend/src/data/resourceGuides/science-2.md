## 1. Cells and the microscope

### Watch: Microscopes and How to Use a Light Microscope

Amoeba Sisters

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tVcEEw6qbBQ)

### Understand it

Focus on why magnification and resolution both matter; the video also introduces microscope types beyond this lesson. Watch its slide preparation as a demonstration, and use only teacher-prepared slides for your own work. A microscope reveals structures too small to see clearly with the naked eye. A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of living organisms. A labelled cell diagram is a simplified model: real cells vary, and a diagram is not a photograph of every cell. 

Typical plant cells have a cell wall outside the membrane; green plant cells also contain chloroplasts. Animal cells do not have a cell wall or chloroplasts. Typical plant and animal cells have a membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus. Bacterial cells differ: they do not have a well-defined nucleus. 

Work with prepared slides only under a teacher's guidance.

### Try it yourself

A drawing shows a membrane, a cell wall and chloroplasts. Is it more consistent with a green plant cell or an animal cell? Name the basic structural and functional unit of living organisms.

**Check:** A green plant cell. The basic structural and functional unit is the cell.

## 2. Levels of organisation

### Watch: Biological Levels in Biology: The World Tour

Amoeba Sisters

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EtWknf1gzKo)

### Understand it

Focus on the video from cells to the whole organism; its later ecosystem levels are an extension. Follow the levels from small to large: cells form tissues, tissues work together in organs, and organs work together in organ systems. The organism is the whole living individual. At each arrow, explain what is being grouped and how the parts work together, rather than only reciting the names.

### Try it yourself

How are a tissue and an organ different?

**Check:** A tissue is a group of similar cells performing a role; an organ contains tissues working together.

## 3. Kinds of microorganisms

### Think it through

Microorganisms include bacteria, many fungi, protozoa and microscopic algae. They are not all harmful: many help make food or recycle materials. Treat viruses separately when studying how they depend on host cells. Compare a specific example and role instead of calling every microbe a germ.

### Try it yourself

Are all microorganisms harmful?

**Check:** No. Many have useful roles in food production, nutrient cycling and other processes.

## 4. Useful microbes and safe practice

### Watch: The beneficial bacteria that make delicious food - Erez Garty

Erez Garty

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eksagPy5tmQ)

### Understand it

Microbes can change food through processes such as fermentation. Yeast produces carbon dioxide that makes bubbles in bread dough, helping it rise. The video gives food examples; connect one of them to the useful change. Do not culture unknown microbes or taste experimental mixtures at home.

### Try it yourself

Why does yeast help bread dough rise?

**Check:** It produces carbon dioxide during fermentation; gas bubbles expand the dough.
