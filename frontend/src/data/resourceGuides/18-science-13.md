## 1. Conditions that support life

### Watch: Why extremophiles bode well for life beyond Earth - Louisa Preston

Louisa Preston

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Bsp5JYNMAQE)

### Understand it

Life as we know it depends on interacting conditions, including liquid water, usable energy and suitable chemical materials. Earth's atmosphere and temperature help maintain environments where these conditions occur. Orbital distance matters, but the same distance from a star does not guarantee the same surface conditions: the star, atmosphere and other features also matter.

Organisms called extremophiles can live in conditions that would harm many familiar organisms. They still depend on resources and particular environmental limits; their existence does not show that any organism can live anywhere. Keep "could support life" separate from "life has been detected". Finding promising conditions is a reason to investigate, not proof of an inhabited world. The video discusses possibilities for a search, not a confirmed discovery of life beyond Earth.

### Try it yourself

In a fictional research exercise, World A orbits a Sun-like star at the same distance as Earth, but its atmosphere and water are unknown. World B has evidence of liquid water and an energy source, but no biological evidence. A pupil says both are definitely inhabited. What can the evidence actually support, and what should be checked next?

**Check:** Neither world is proven inhabited by these facts. A's orbital distance alone does not establish its temperature, atmosphere or water conditions. B has promising ingredients, not a detection of life. Investigate further environmental conditions and seek independent biological evidence while checking non-living explanations. Possibility and confirmed evidence are different claims.

## 2. Earth systems and their connections

### Watch: Real World: Earth Systems

NASA eClips

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o8K0RTYM6Mc)

### Understand it

Earth's land, water, air and living organisms form connected systems. The geosphere includes rocks and soil; the hydrosphere includes water; the atmosphere is the surrounding air; the biosphere includes living organisms. These labels help organise a model, but they are not sealed boxes. Water can move from soil through a plant and into the air.

Follow a specific link rather than only naming systems. Roots can hold soil, so removing vegetation may leave more soil exposed to being carried by rain into a stream. That connects a living component, land and moving water. The amount depends on conditions such as slope, rainfall and ground cover; a possible chain is not proof that every cleared patch has the same result.

### Try it yourself

A hillside loses much of its vegetation. After heavy rain, a nearby stream becomes muddier. Name three Earth systems that could be linked and explain a possible sequence. Does one muddy-water observation alone prove that the vegetation loss was the only cause?

**Check:** The biosphere includes the lost plants, the geosphere includes the exposed soil, and the hydrosphere includes rain and stream water. Less root and ground cover may allow more soil to be carried into the stream. Other sources of sediment and rainfall changes could also matter, so one observation does not establish a single cause. Compare conditions and repeated observations before drawing that conclusion.

## 3. Continuity of life and reproduction

### Watch: A new plant from part of a parent plant

Khan Academy India - English

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p4652vo5Oic)

Use vegetative propagation as one example of reproduction: a suitable part of a parent plant can develop into a separate new plant. This differs from an existing plant simply growing more leaves or a longer branch. Not every plant part can form a complete new plant; suitable parts and conditions depend on the species. The video focuses on plants, not every form of reproduction. Then separate an individual staying alive from a population continuing across generations. Producing new individuals does not guarantee that a population increases; compare new individuals with deaths in the model below.

### Understand it

Individual survival and the continuation of a population are different questions. A plant can stay alive through growth, respiration and obtaining resources without producing a new plant in that period. Reproduction produces new individuals, allowing a population to continue as older individuals die. Growth of one individual is not itself reproduction.

For example, a cutting that develops into a separate new plant is a form of vegetative reproduction, whereas an existing branch simply getting longer is growth. Producing new individuals does not guarantee a population will increase: compare births or new plants with deaths, and consider movement into or out of the area. For a simple count with no movement, next population = starting population + new individuals - deaths.

### Try it yourself

A closed model population starts with 24 plants. During one interval, 7 new plants establish and 9 existing plants die, with no plants entering or leaving. Find the final population. A surviving plant also grows two new leaves. Does that leaf growth count as two new plants, and does reproduction guarantee population growth?

**Check:** 24 + 7 - 9 = 22 plants. Two leaves on an existing plant are growth of that individual, not two new plants. Reproduction added individuals, but deaths exceeded new plants in this model, so the population still fell. An individual can remain alive without reproducing during the interval; population continuity is a separate question.

## 4. Threats and protection

### Watch: Maintaining biodiversity

Cognito

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bs9e6ovISbs)

Use the video to connect protecting species with protecting their habitats. It introduces conservation choices, not a wetland restoration procedure or a guarantee that one action removes every threat. Specific laws and farming choices depend on place and conditions. When considering a wetland, match each action to its pressure and check what remains unresolved. Do not catch, feed or disturb wildlife to test an idea.

### Understand it

Protecting life means protecting the conditions and relationships that species need, not only a named animal. A wetland bird needs suitable feeding areas, water conditions and places to rest or breed. Protecting the bird while allowing its wetland to be polluted or drained can leave those needs unmet. Habitat loss, pollution and climate change can interact.

Match an action to the pressure it addresses. Reducing litter helps with one kind of waste, but it does not by itself stop an ongoing polluted-water discharge. Wider protection can require managing water, land use and pollution sources as well as individual care. Compare evidence about the source of harm before deciding which action is enough. Observe wildlife from a safe distance; do not catch, feed or disturb animals to test a conservation idea.

### Try it yourself

A wetland clean-up removes visible plastic, but dirty water continues flowing in from a drain and the feeding habitat is shrinking. Give one benefit of the clean-up and two unresolved pressures. What would you check before claiming the wetland is now protected?

**Check:** Removing plastic reduces visible litter and some related hazards. The continuing polluted-water inflow and habitat loss remain unresolved. Check the inflow source and water conditions, the area and quality of usable habitat, and changes in the organisms over time. One clean-up is useful, but it is not evidence that every pressure has been removed.
