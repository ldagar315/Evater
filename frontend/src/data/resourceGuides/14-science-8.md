## 1. Mixtures and separation

### Watch: Filtration, Evaporation & Crystallisation

Cognito

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vi_SJBnxmHo)

### Understand it

Imagine a mixture of sand, dissolved salt and water. A suitable filter traps the insoluble sand, but the salt stays dissolved in the water that passes through. The clear filtrate is still a mixture: looking clear does not prove that it is a pure substance.

If the aim is to recover salt, water can be removed by evaporation, leaving the salt. That step does not collect the water; recovering water as well needs a method that collects its vapour and condenses it. Choose each step for a physical difference and name what it recovers. Treat this as a paper reasoning example, not an instruction to heat mixtures at home; any practical work needs the teacher's approved setup and supervision.

### Try it yourself

A student filters salty water containing sand and calls the clear liquid "pure water". Explain the mistake. If the only goal is to obtain the sand, is evaporating that liquid necessary?

**Check:** Dissolved salt passes through with the water, so the filtrate is not pure water. The filter has already separated the sand; evaporating the filtrate is not needed just to obtain sand.

## 2. Elements and compounds

### Watch: Elements, compounds and mixtures

Freesciencelessons

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nUzOXy9V-K0)

### Understand it

Count kinds of atoms, not just the number of atoms in a particle. Oxygen gas contains molecules made of two oxygen atoms, but only one kind of atom, so oxygen is an element. A water molecule contains hydrogen and oxygen atoms chemically combined in a fixed ratio, so water is a compound. Having two atoms does not by itself make something a compound.

A mixture is different again: salt dissolved in water contains physically mixed substances. Evaporating the water can separate salt from that mixture, but it does not split the salt itself into its constituent elements. Likewise, boiling water changes its state; it does not turn it into hydrogen and oxygen. These distinctions concern composition, not whether a sample looks clean.

### Try it yourself

Classify each description and give a reason: (a) a pure gas whose molecules each contain two nitrogen atoms; (b) a pure substance whose particles contain carbon and oxygen chemically combined in a fixed ratio; (c) water with dissolved sugar. Does boiling (c) to remove water split the sugar into its elements?

**Check:** (a) Element: one kind of atom. (b) Compound: different elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio. (c) Mixture: sugar and water are physically mixed. Removing water is a physical separation, not decomposition of sugar into its elements.

## 3. Pure substances and mixtures

### Watch: The science of macaroni salad: What's in a mixture? - Josh Kurz

Josh Kurz

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vt7lN4QPU0k)

### Understand it

In chemistry, a pure substance has a definite composition and is one chemical substance. It can be an element, such as copper, or a compound, such as pure water. Water contains hydrogen and oxygen chemically combined; having two elements does not make it a mixture. A mixture contains more than one substance and can have varying proportions.

Uniform appearance is not enough to prove purity. Clear salt solution is a mixture because salt and water remain different substances, even when no salt grains are visible. Separating dissolved salt from water is a physical separation of a mixture; separating water into its constituent elements needs chemical change. These are classification examples, not instructions to heat or electrolyse substances at home.

### Try it yourself

Classify a sample containing only silver, a sample containing only carbon dioxide, and a well-mixed sample of air as element, compound or mixture. A learner says air must be pure because it looks uniform. Explain the error and identify which two samples can be pure substances.

**Check:** Silver is an element. Pure carbon dioxide is a compound because carbon and oxygen are chemically combined. Air is a mixture of gases, even when uniform. The silver and carbon dioxide samples can each be a pure substance; uniform appearance alone cannot distinguish a pure substance from a homogeneous mixture.

## 4. Metals, non-metals, alloys and minerals

### Think it through

Keep different classification questions separate. Aluminium is a metallic element and sulfur is a non-metallic element. An alloy is a mixture involving at least one metal: bronze commonly combines copper and tin. Mixing metals does not turn the result into a new element, and uniform appearance does not give an alloy a fixed compound formula. Do not classify a material from shininess alone.

A mineral is a naturally occurring solid, not simply another word for metal. Many minerals are compounds, such as quartz, while native copper is an elemental mineral. Rocks can contain several minerals. Thus "element or mixture?" and "mineral or manufactured material?" ask different questions; one label cannot replace the other.

### Try it yourself

Consider a manufactured uniform copper-zinc alloy, a sample of pure aluminium, a natural quartz crystal, and a rock explicitly containing several minerals. Classify their chemical makeup. Would calling the quartz a mineral mean it must be a metal, or would calling the alloy uniform mean it must be an element?

**Check:** The copper-zinc alloy is brass, a mixture. Pure aluminium is an element. Quartz is a compound mineral. The stated multi-mineral rock is a mixture. A mineral need not be a metal, and an alloy remains a mixture even when it looks uniform. Native elemental minerals also show why not every mineral must be a compound.
