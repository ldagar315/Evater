## 1. Mixtures and separation

### Think it through

A mixture contains substances physically combined. Choose a separation method using a property that differs between them. A suitable filter can trap sand from water, but dissolved salt travels through with the water. Explain why the method fits this mixture rather than saying filtering removes everything.

### Try it yourself

Why can filtering remove sand from water but not ordinary dissolved salt?

**Check:** Sand particles are trapped by a suitable filter; dissolved salt passes through with the water.

## 2. Elements and compounds

### Think it through

An element contains one kind of atom. A compound has elements chemically combined in a fixed composition. Water is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen, not an element. Compare what the substance is made of with whether its components are merely mixed or chemically combined.

### Try it yourself

Is water an element?

**Check:** No. It is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen.

## 3. Pure substances and mixtures

### Understand it

In science, 'pure substance' refers to composition, not simply cleanliness or usefulness. Air is a mixture of gases even when it is clean enough to breathe. The video explores mixtures through food; use it to practise distinguishing physically combined ingredients from a single substance.

### Try it yourself

Is air a pure substance?

**Check:** No. It is a mixture of gases.

## 4. Metals, non-metals, alloys and minerals

### Think it through

An alloy is a mixture, often involving metals; brass is commonly made from copper and zinc. It is not an element. A mineral is not simply another word for a metal. Make a table of named examples and explain the reason for each category rather than grouping by shiny appearance alone.

### Try it yourself

Is brass an element?

**Check:** No. Brass is an alloy, commonly of copper and zinc.
