Pick one topic at a time. Watch, work through the example, then hide the answer and have a go yourself.

## 1. Compare quantities with ratios

### Watch: Equivalent ratios

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eb-GHXCqkhQ)

### Understand it

A ratio compares quantities in a stated order. With 8 red beads and 12 blue beads, red : blue = 8 : 12 = 2 : 3. Divide both parts by the same nonzero number to keep the comparison. The simplified ratio describes equal-sized groups, not necessarily the actual number of beads. Reversing the order gives blue : red = 3 : 2.

Red : all beads is different: it is 8 : 20 = 2 : 5, since the total includes both colours. Scaling both parts preserves a ratio, but adding the same amount usually does not. For example, 2 : 3 and 4 : 5 are not equivalent: 2 × 5 and 3 × 4 are unequal.

### Try it yourself

A bag contains 18 green and 30 yellow counters. Find green : yellow and green : all counters. Someone adds six of each colour and says the green : yellow ratio is unchanged because the additions match. Test the claim without judging by the drawing.

**Check:** Green : yellow = 18 : 30 = 3 : 5. Green : all = 18 : 48 = 3 : 8; the second term is the total, not just yellow. After the additions, green : yellow = 24 : 36 = 2 : 3, which is not 3 : 5 since 2 × 5 ≠ 3 × 3. Equal additions do not multiply both original quantities by the same factor. Also, 3 : 5 never meant there were only eight counters in the original bag.

## 2. Put the units right first

### Watch: Equivalent ratios

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eb-GHXCqkhQ)

### Understand it

Before comparing two lengths or two masses, put them in the same unit. A 75 cm model compared with a 3 m object gives model : object = 75 : 300 = 1 : 4 after expressing both lengths in centimetres. Writing 75 : 3 compares unlike units as though they were the same. The shorter length should have the smaller ratio part.

Decimals do not change the principle. For 0.8 kg : 300 g, first write 800 g : 300 g, then simplify to 8 : 3. Keep the order written in the question. If converting to metres instead, 0.75 m : 3 m still gives 1 : 4; choosing a different common unit must not change the ratio.

### Try it yourself

Two ribbons are A = 1.2 m and B = 80 cm. Find A : B in simplest form using centimetres, then check using metres. A pupil reports 1.2 : 80 = 3 : 200. Identify the missing step. If A stays the same length but B is doubled, what is the new A : B?

**Check:** In centimetres, 120 : 80 = 3 : 2. In metres, 1.2 : 0.8 also gives 3 : 2 after multiplying both terms by 10 and simplifying. The pupil simplified the numbers before matching their units; 3 : 200 cannot describe the longer ribbon as the larger part. Doubling only B gives 120 : 160 = 3 : 4. The ratio changes because only one length was scaled.

## 3. Find the value of one

### Watch: Direct variation

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rSadG6EtJmY)

### Understand it

The unitary method finds one unit first, then scales to the required quantity. At a constant price, 4 identical notebooks costing ₹60 means ₹60 ÷ 4 = ₹15 per notebook, so 7 cost 7 × ₹15 = ₹105. The model assumes every notebook has the same price and no quantity discount.

A fixed delivery charge is separate from that constant item rate. If the four notebooks instead have a total bill of ₹80 including one ₹20 delivery charge, subtract the fixed charge first: (₹80 - ₹20) ÷ 4 = ₹15 per notebook. For seven notebooks, add delivery once: ₹105 + ₹20 = ₹125. Scaling the entire bill would scale a charge that should stay fixed.

### Try it yourself

A fictional order for five identical folders totals ₹170, including a single ₹20 delivery charge. Assume a constant folder price and the same single delivery charge for any order size. Find the bill for eight folders. Explain why (₹170 ÷ 5) × 8 gives the wrong bill. Would the final bill always double when the folder count doubles?

**Check:** The five folders alone cost ₹170 - ₹20 = ₹150, so one folder costs ₹30. Eight folders cost ₹240, and adding delivery once gives ₹260. The proposed method gives ₹272 because it spreads the fixed charge into a ₹34 average and scales that average. Doubling the count from five to ten gives ₹300 + ₹20 = ₹320, not ₹340. The item cost doubles, but the one fixed charge does not.

## 4. Share an amount unequally

### Watch: Sharing a total in a ratio

Corbettmaths

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cflZnf9H5l4)

### Understand it

To share ₹600 in the ratio 2 : 3, add the parts: 2 + 3 = 5. One equal part is ₹600 ÷ 5 = ₹120, giving shares ₹240 and ₹360. The first share is 2/5 of the whole, not 2/3 of it. Check both that the shares add to ₹600 and that ₹240 : ₹360 simplifies to 2 : 3.

A difference between shares is not the total. In a 2 : 3 share, the difference is one part, while the total is five parts. Decide what the given amount represents before dividing. The same ratio can describe many different totals; one actual amount fixes the scale.

### Try it yourself

Two teams share an imaginary prize in the ratio A : B = 4 : 7. Team B receives ₹270 more than team A. Find the two shares and the total prize. A pupil divides ₹270 by 11 to find one part. Explain why that uses the wrong quantity, then check both the ratio and the stated difference.

**Check:** The difference is 7 - 4 = 3 equal parts, so one part is ₹270 ÷ 3 = ₹90. A receives 4 × ₹90 = ₹360; B receives 7 × ₹90 = ₹630. The total is ₹990. Checks: ₹360 : ₹630 = 4 : 7, and ₹630 - ₹360 = ₹270. Dividing ₹270 by 11 would treat the difference as if it were the entire prize. The total really is 11 parts, but the question did not give that total.
