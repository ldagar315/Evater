## 1. Solute, solvent and solution

### Think it through

In salt water, salt is the solute, water is the solvent, and the mixture is a solution. Dissolving spreads the solute through the solvent; it does not make the matter vanish. Explain where the salt has gone using a particle picture rather than saying it has disappeared.

### Try it yourself

In a simple salt-water solution, identify solute and solvent.

**Check:** Salt is the solute; water is the solvent.

## 2. Saturation and solubility

### Think it through

A saturated solution has reached its dissolving limit for a stated temperature and amount of solvent. Adding more solute after that can leave some undissolved. When comparing solutions, state those conditions so you do not confuse more dissolved material with more material simply sitting at the bottom.

### Try it yourself

Can adding more solute always produce a more concentrated solution?

**Check:** No. Once saturated under the stated conditions, extra solute can remain undissolved.

## 3. Temperature and dissolved gases

### Think it through

Do not use one temperature rule for every substance. Dissolved gases commonly become less soluble as water warms; the behaviour of a solid solute can differ. Compare the same substance under stated conditions and describe the result, rather than claiming that all substances dissolve better in hot water.

### Try it yourself

Why should you be cautious about claiming all substances dissolve better in warmer water?

**Check:** Solubility depends on the substance; dissolved gases commonly become less soluble as water warms.

## 4. Density, floating and sinking

### Understand it

Density is mass divided by volume. A 60 g sample occupying 20 cm³ has density 3 g/cm³. Floating depends on the relevant average densities and buoyancy, not total mass alone. The video explains ice floating in water; use that example without assuming every solid floats in its liquid.

### Try it yourself

A sample has mass 60 g and volume 20 cm³. Find its density.

**Check:** 3 g/cm³.
