## 1. Solute, solvent and solution

### Watch: Identify the solute, solvent and solution

Khan Academy India - English

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kYgGI9Ox3gU)

Use the sugar-water example to name what dissolves, what does the dissolving, and the whole uniform mixture. Dissolving does not mean the solute has stopped existing. The syrup example shows why the solute is not always the smaller amount. Water can dissolve many substances, but not everything. If no matter enters or leaves, total mass is conserved; that does not require the separate volumes to add exactly. Then use the original explanation and question below. Do not taste laboratory solutions.

### Understand it

In salt water, salt is the solute, water is the solvent, and the mixture is a solution. Dissolving spreads the solute through the solvent; it does not make the matter vanish. Explain where the salt has gone using a particle picture rather than saying it has disappeared.

### Try it yourself

A spoonful of salt dissolves completely in water. What are the solute, solvent and solution, and has the salt stopped existing?

**Check:** Salt is the solute, water is the solvent, and salt water is the solution. The salt is still present, spread through the water.

### Extend your reasoning

In sugar water, sugar is the solute, water is the solvent, and their uniform mixture is the solution. Dissolving spreads the solute through the solvent; it does not destroy its matter. The solute can be present even when separate grains are no longer visible. A solution is the whole mixture, not another name for the solvent alone.

Keep mass and volume claims separate. If nothing spills or escapes, 5 g of sugar mixed with 95 g of water gives 100 g of solution. That does not mean their separate volumes must add exactly. Use mass conservation and a particle picture rather than saying the sugar has disappeared. Do not taste laboratory solutions or use tasting as a test.

### Try an additional case

A sealed vessel contains 72 g of water and 8 g of a soluble solid. The solid dissolves fully, with no material entering or leaving. Name the solute, solvent and solution, then find the mass of the contents. A learner predicts only 72 g because the grains are no longer visible. Explain the error. Must the final volume equal the sum of the separate starting volumes?

**Reasoning:** The solid is the solute, water the solvent, and the uniform mixture the solution. The contents have mass 72 + 8 = 80 g; invisible grains do not mean missing matter. Mass conservation does not require the final volume to be exactly the sum of the separate volumes. The vessel's own mass would be added only if the question asked for vessel plus contents.

## 2. Saturation and solubility

### Watch: solutions tutorial - unsaturated, saturated, supersaturated

Ms. Heather Moser

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JQRIlS9lNdg)

### Understand it

A saturated solution has reached its dissolving limit for a stated temperature and amount of solvent. It can still look clear: solid need not remain if exactly the limiting amount has dissolved. Undissolved extra solid shows that more was added than could dissolve under those conditions. The video's later supersaturation example is an extension, not needed here.

For an invented solute, suppose 40 g of water can dissolve at most 8 g at 20°C. Adding 10 g and allowing enough time leaves 8 g dissolved and 2 g undissolved. More stirring can help reach the limit, but cannot raise it at unchanged conditions. Adding solvent is different: more water provides more dissolving capacity at the same temperature. Do not taste laboratory solutions.

### Try it yourself

Use fictional data, not a real chemical recipe: at 25°C, 50 g of water can dissolve at most 12 g of solid X. You add 16 g and allow the system to settle at that temperature. How much dissolves and remains? Then add another 50 g of water, keep 25°C and allow enough mixing time. Is the resulting solution saturated, and how much more X could dissolve?

**Check:** With the first 50 g of water, 12 g dissolves and 16 - 12 = 4 g remains undissolved. Doubling the water to 100 g doubles the stated capacity to 24 g. All 16 g can now dissolve, leaving capacity for 24 - 16 = 8 g more, so the final solution is unsaturated. Extra water changed the capacity; stirring alone at the original conditions would not. Clear appearance by itself cannot tell you whether the solution is exactly at its limit.

## 3. Temperature and dissolved gases

### Watch: How temperature affects gas solubility

Lab Hamster

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wK4reyh86w0)

Watch the cold, room-temperature and warm seltzer demonstration without copying it. Opening a bottle also lowers the gas pressure, so fizz alone is not a controlled measurement of the temperature effect. Use the fixed-pressure comparison below, and do not heat sealed bottles or repeat this demonstration.

### Understand it

Do not use one temperature rule for every substance. Many gases become less soluble in water as temperature rises, when the pressure of that gas above the water stays the same. The behaviour of a solid solute can differ. Dissolving faster is not the same as increasing the maximum amount dissolved after enough time.

Pressure is another variable for dissolved gases. To test a temperature effect, compare the same gas, the same amount of water and the same pressure of that gas, after each reaches equilibrium. If both temperature and pressure change, a different dissolved amount cannot be attributed to temperature alone. Use the fictional data below on paper; do not heat or open sealed gas containers to try this.

### Try it yourself

For the same fictional gas and 100 g of water, equilibrium limits are A: 10°C, 100 kPa gas pressure, 8 mg dissolved; B: 30°C, 100 kPa, 5 mg; C: 30°C, 200 kPa, 10 mg. Which pair isolates temperature and what does it show? Which pair isolates pressure? A learner compares A with C and concludes warming always increases gas solubility. Why is that not justified?

**Check:** A and B keep gas pressure and water amount fixed while temperature rises; the limit falls from 8 mg to 5 mg. B and C keep temperature fixed while gas pressure rises; the limit rises from 5 mg to 10 mg in these data. A and C change both variables, so their difference cannot establish a temperature-only effect. One fictional data set also cannot prove an "always" rule for every substance. All three are equilibrium limits, not measurements of dissolving speed.

## 4. Density, floating and sinking

### Watch: Why does ice float in water? - George Zaidan and Charles Morton

George Zaidan and Charles Morton

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UukRgqzk-KE)

### Understand it

Density is mass divided by volume. A 60 g sample occupying 20 cm³ has density 3 g/cm³. Floating depends on the relevant average densities and buoyancy, not total mass alone. The video explains ice floating in water; use that example without assuming every solid floats in its liquid.

### Try it yourself

A sample has mass 60 g and volume 20 cm³. Find its density.

**Check:** 3 g/cm³.

### Work through an example

Use supplied values for a fictional object: mass 0.24 kg and outer volume 300 cm³. Convert mass first: 0.24 kg = 240 g. Its average density is 240 ÷ 300 = 0.8 g/cm³, not 0.0008 g/cm³. A separate 30 g solid sample occupying 10 cm³ has density 3 g/cm³. The heavier object has the lower average density because its volume is much larger. For a hollow object, use its total mass and whole outer volume, not only the density of its wall material.

### Try a different case

In a simplified paper model, sealed rigid object A has mass 90 g and outer volume 150 cm³; solid object B has mass 20 g and volume 10 cm³. Water has density 1 g/cm³. Ignore surface tension and other support; assume neither object absorbs water, dissolves or changes shape. Calculate their average densities and predict which can float. Does the smaller mass decide?

**Reasoning:** A has average density 90 ÷ 150 = 0.6 g/cm³, below the stated water density, so it can float partly submerged in this model. B has density 20 ÷ 10 = 2 g/cm³ and sinks despite being lighter. Compare mass per volume, not mass alone. A floating at rest has buoyancy balancing its weight, not zero gravity. Keep the stated assumptions; this is not an instruction to test unknown materials in water.

### Extend your reasoning

Density is mass divided by volume, with matching units. A 60 g sample occupying 20 cm³ has density 3 g/cm³. Doubling both mass and volume gives 120 g ÷ 40 cm³ = 3 g/cm³, so a larger mass does not automatically mean a larger density. Compare equal volumes, or calculate the ratio.

For a simple object free of supports and trapped surface effects, compare its overall average density with the surrounding liquid: an object less dense than the liquid can float, while one more dense tends to sink. A hollow object's average density includes its enclosed space. The video explains ice floating in water; it does not imply that every solid floats in its own liquid.

### Try an additional case

Object A has mass 54 g and volume 60 cm³; object B has mass 40 g and volume 20 cm³. Calculate both densities. In water of density 1.0 g/cm³, use the simple average-density model to predict which can float. Which object has greater mass, and why does that not settle floating?

**Reasoning:** A has density 54/60 = 0.9 g/cm³, while B has 40/20 = 2.0 g/cm³. A is less dense than the water and can float; B is more dense and tends to sink under this model. A has the greater mass, but also a much larger volume. Density, not mass alone, is the relevant comparison.
