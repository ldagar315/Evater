## 1. Observations, questions and explanations

### Watch: Look Who's Talking: Crash Course Kids #27.2

Crash Course Kids

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=La0m_ocR7cQ)

### Understand it

An observation describes what you notice or measure; an explanation proposes why it happens. 'The plant bends toward the window' reports a visible pattern, while 'the plant wants sunlight' adds an untested explanation. A useful question connects something you could change with an outcome you could record. Plants convert light energy into chemical energy; they do not create energy.

Separate the record from your interpretation. Two objects differing in an outcome do not by themselves tell you which of their many differences caused it. Write a possible explanation as a proposal to test, and consider what further observation would distinguish it from another explanation.

### Try it yourself

Two plants of the same species are measured after a week: A is 14 cm tall and B is 11 cm tall. A learner writes, 'A is taller because it received more light.' Which part is a measured observation, and which part is an explanation? The record does not include light exposure. Name one extra factor to check before attributing the height difference to light.

**Check:** The measured observation is the 14 cm versus 11 cm height, a 3 cm difference. More light causing the difference is an explanation, not a recorded fact about exposure. Check starting heights, for example; a plant that began taller could still finish taller without growing more. Water supply or other conditions could also differ. State what is known separately from what needs testing.

## 2. Fair comparisons and variables

### Think it through

A fair comparison changes one relevant factor while keeping other relevant conditions comparable. For a dissolving-time comparison, define the finish point, such as no visible grains remaining under the same observation method. Write the factor changed, the outcome measured and the conditions kept constant before interpreting the results.

To compare temperatures, keep the substance, solute mass, water volume, particle size and stirring method the same. Otherwise, a shorter time could have more than one explanation. Repeating comparable trials helps check whether a pattern persists. This is a planning exercise, not an instruction to handle hot water.

### Try it yourself

A record compares 5 g of fine sugar stirred in 100 mL of warmer water with 10 g of sugar cubes left still in 50 mL of cooler water. The first dissolves sooner. Does this isolate temperature's effect? Redesign the comparison by naming the changed factor, measured outcome and at least four conditions to keep comparable.

**Check:** No: mass, particle size, stirring and water volume also differ. Change only water temperature and measure time to the same defined dissolving endpoint. Use the same solute, solute mass, particle size, water volume and stirring method, with comparable containers and repeated trials. The original result does not identify temperature alone as the reason for the faster dissolving.

## 3. Measuring, recording and patterns

### Watch: What's the difference between accuracy and precision? - Matt Anticole

Matt Anticole

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hRAFPdDppzs)

### Understand it

Record measurements with units and repeat them to check consistency. Precision concerns agreement among repeated readings; accuracy concerns closeness to a known or accepted value. Readings can be tightly clustered yet inaccurate if a measuring device has an offset. More repeats do not automatically remove that offset.

Use a known reference when one is available and record a suspected measurement problem rather than hiding it. Averaging can help describe repeated results, but it cannot by itself turn a consistently biased instrument into a correct one. Keep the original readings so someone else can inspect the pattern.

### Try it yourself

A reference object has an accepted mass of 100 g. One balance reports 104 g, 104 g and 104 g. Are these readings consistent with one another, and are they close to the reference? A learner proposes taking 20 more readings and averaging them without checking the balance. What problem could remain?

**Check:** The readings agree exactly, so they are precise in this record, but each is 4 g above the accepted value. A possible systematic offset could remain even after more repeats. Recheck the instrument and method against the reference rather than assuming averaging fixes the bias. Consistency and accuracy answer different questions.

## 4. Evidence, conclusions and revising ideas

### Watch: Look Who's Talking: Crash Course Kids #27.2

Crash Course Kids

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=La0m_ocR7cQ)

### Understand it

A conclusion should answer the question using the evidence actually collected. A pattern in a few trials can support an explanation under those conditions without proving it always holds. Keep an unexpected reading, examine how it was obtained and plan a further check. Do not delete a result simply because it disagrees with your prediction.

Give the limits as well as the pattern: how many trials, which conditions and what was measured. Revising an explanation after a fair check is part of science. In the plant example, keep the energy language correct: plants convert light energy into chemical energy, not make energy from nothing.

### Try it yourself

A teacher-provided record lists four trials under each of two conditions. A takes 28, 29, 30 and 31 seconds; B takes 24, 25, 26 and 52 seconds. A learner deletes 52 only because it spoils the expected pattern and claims B is always faster. What should stay in the record, and what conclusion or follow-up is justified?

**Check:** Keep all readings, including 52, unless a separately documented measurement or recording error justifies a correction. B is faster in three trials but slower in one; the record does not support 'always faster'. Check the unusual trial's method and repeat comparable trials. Report the mixed result and its limits instead of choosing only the readings that fit.
