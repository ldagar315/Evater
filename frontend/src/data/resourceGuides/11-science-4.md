## 1. Cells, circuits and current

### Watch: Simple Circuits

FuseSchool - Global Education

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ij4dVhprZlw)

### Understand it

A simple cell-powered lamp circuit needs a complete conducting path from one cell terminal, through its components and back to the other terminal. Trace the loop, not just whether parts look close together. A break in a series path prevents current around that path. Focus on the cell, switch and lamp in the video; its ammeter and voltmeter details are extra.

In a voltaic cell, chemical reactions involving different electrodes and an electrolyte supply electrical energy. A cell is an energy source, not a box that stores current. A closed loop is necessary but does not guarantee that every real circuit works: cell condition, contact quality and a working lamp also matter. Reason from diagrams; do not connect an experiment to a wall socket.

### Try it yourself

Diagram A shows one wire from a cell terminal to a lamp, but no return path to the other terminal. Diagram B adds the return wire but leaves a series switch open. For each, explain why the ideal simple lamp circuit stays off. If a real version of B stays dark after its switch closes, name one further check rather than concluding a complete path is never needed.

**Check:** A lacks a complete return path; B has a break at the open switch. Neither gives current a complete path around the ideal circuit. After closing B, a weak cell, poor contact or faulty lamp could still prevent lighting. A complete path is necessary, not a promise that all components work. Use teacher-approved low-voltage equipment only under guidance, never a wall socket.

## 2. Magnetic effect of electric current

### Watch: How does an Electromagnet Work?

Science Buddies

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cxELqN7wjS0)

### Understand it

A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field that can deflect a nearby compass. The compass needle is itself a small magnet; its direction responds to the fields around it. Keep wire, current and compass as separate parts of your explanation. Current direction is not another name for a north or south pole.

Watch the video's compass demonstration only. Do not copy the paper electromagnet shown: it can overheat and catch fire. To interpret a recorded switch-on/switch-off comparison, keep the compass and wire positions comparable and consider nearby magnets as well. The wire's current-produced field stops when its current stops; other magnetic fields need not disappear.

### Try it yourself

A teacher's diagram shows a compass near a fixed wire. With the switch open, the needle points in its original direction; with current, it turns; when current stops, it returns. A learner says switching off has removed every magnetic field from the room. What does the record support, and what part of the claim goes too far?

**Check:** The change is consistent with a magnetic field produced by the current affecting the compass. Switching off removes that current-produced contribution, not Earth's field or every other possible field. The compass can still point in an original direction. Watch only: do not copy the paper electromagnet because of overheating and fire risk.

## 3. Electromagnets and their uses

### Watch: How does an Electromagnet Work?

Science Buddies

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cxELqN7wjS0)

### Understand it

A coil carrying current acts as an electromagnet. A suitable iron core can strengthen it, and current control makes it useful in devices that pick up or release magnetic materials. Follow the chain: current in coil, magnetic effect, attraction of a suitable object, then the device's movement. It does not attract every material merely because it is called metal.

The field of the powered coil depends on its current. Real iron cores may retain some magnetism after switch-off, so do not turn a simple model into a promise of perfect release in every device. Watch only; do not copy the video's paper electromagnet, which can overheat and catch fire. Disconnect school equipment as your teacher directs.

### Try it yourself

A model lifting device uses a current-carrying coil with a suitable iron core to pick up iron pieces. Predict the intended effect of switching the current off and explain why a permanent magnet would not offer the same direct current control. Does this reasoning prove that every metal object will be lifted, or that a real core can never retain any magnetism?

**Check:** Switching off removes the powered coil's magnetic field so the device is intended to release the load. A permanent magnet is not switched off just by breaking a separate electric circuit. The argument concerns suitable magnetic materials, not every metal. A real core can retain some magnetism, so release behaviour needs checking rather than an absolute guarantee. This is a diagram exercise, not a build instruction.

## 4. Heating effect and electrical safety

### Watch: How electric current produces heating

Elearnin

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zzmHjg0PORY)

Use the animation to distinguish energy transferred by heating from a device's intended useful output. A fan can warm up even though its intended job is moving air; a heater uses the heating effect deliberately. The later equations are an extension, not needed for the question below. When comparing heating, keep the other conditions fixed: increasing resistance does not always increase heating if the current also changes. Watch the diagrams only, not as instructions to build a heater, touch-test a wire or open an appliance.

### Understand it

Current through a resistive element can transfer electrical energy into heating. In an electric heater this is intended; in a connection or lead, excessive heating can be unwanted and dangerous. The heating effect is not limited to components that visibly glow. Material, dimensions, current and duration can all matter, so 'more heat' is not a universal rule based on only one feature.

Study mains-powered devices through diagrams only. Do not open appliances, touch an element to test it, or connect an experiment to a wall socket. For school equipment, follow teacher-approved low-voltage instructions and stop as directed if cells or wires overheat. Useful heating and safe operation are different questions.

### Try it yourself

Two diagram captions read: 'A heater's element warming during normal use' and 'An appliance lead becoming unusually hot'. A learner labels both safe because they show the same heating effect. Explain the flaw. Would a lead that is not visibly glowing automatically be safe to touch or keep using?

**Check:** The same physical effect can be useful in an intended heating element but hazardous in an overheating lead. Not glowing does not establish a safe temperature or safe operation. Do not touch-test, open or repair the appliance; keep clear and alert a responsible adult. A shared explanation of heating is not permission to use an unsafe device.
