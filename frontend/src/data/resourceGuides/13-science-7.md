## 1. The particle model of matter

### Watch: Representing solids, liquids, and gases using particulate models

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Q45iIeMdCM)

### Understand it

Matter can be modelled as tiny particles with spaces between them. Particle diagrams help explain observations; their circles are symbols, not visible balls or magnified holes in a material. Ask what each part of a diagram stands for before using it to explain a change. Focus on the particle diagrams; the later polymer and bonding examples are extra, not needed for this first model.

### Try it yourself

Do gaps between particles mean matter contains visible holes?

**Check:** No. Particle spacing is a microscopic model feature.

### Extend your reasoning

Matter can be modelled as extremely small constituent particles with spaces between them. A drawn circle stands for a particle; it is not a photograph of a tiny solid ball. The white space between circles represents separation in the model, not visible holes or little pockets that must be filled with air. A grain of powder itself contains very many constituent particles.

Use the diagram's key before making a claim. If a diagram uses one circle to represent many particles, six circles do not mean that a real sample contains only six particles. Enlarging a picture changes the drawing, not the sample. Focus on the video's particle models; later polymer and bonding examples are extra.

### Try an additional case

A model shows eight equal circles spaced through a sealed box. Its key says each circle represents many particles. A learner says the box contains exactly eight particles and the gaps are air bubbles. Which claims go beyond the model? If the picture is enlarged, has the actual sample expanded?

**Reasoning:** Both claims go beyond the key: each circle represents many particles, and interparticle spaces are not automatically air bubbles. Enlarging the drawing does not expand the real sample. Keep the representation separate from the material it models.

## 2. Solids, liquids and gases

### Watch: Representing solids, liquids, and gases using particulate models

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Q45iIeMdCM)

### Understand it

Compare arrangement and motion as well as spacing. Particles in a solid vibrate rather than staying completely motionless; liquid particles can move past one another, and gas particles are much farther apart. The larger spaces in a gas help explain why it is usually easier to compress. Focus on the diagrams before the later, more advanced examples.

### Try it yourself

Why is a gas generally easier to compress than a liquid?

**Check:** Gas particles are much farther apart, leaving more space to reduce.

### Extend your reasoning

Compare arrangement, motion and spacing, not only whether a material flows. In a solid, constituent particles vibrate about positions rather than being completely motionless. In a liquid they remain close but can move past one another. Gas particles are much farther apart and move through the available space, so reducing those spaces usually makes a gas much easier to compress than a liquid. Compression does not require the particles themselves to shrink.

A collection of solid grains can pour even though each grain keeps its own shape. Flow of the whole collection does not by itself make each grain a liquid. Use the particle model together with observations about the material, not a single everyday word. Focus on the video's diagrams before its more advanced examples.

### Try an additional case

Two sealed model boxes contain the same number of identical gas particles. Box B has half the volume of Box A, under stated conditions that keep both samples gaseous. Should the diagram for B show half as many particles, smaller particles, or closer spacing? A learner also says every material that can be poured must be a liquid. Give a counterexample.

**Reasoning:** Show the same particle count and particle type with less space between them. Neither losing particles nor shrinking their drawn size is the intended compression model. Dry beads can be poured as a collection while each bead remains solid, so pouring alone does not identify a liquid. Particles in solids still vibrate.

## 3. Diffusion and particle motion

### Watch: Diffusion of Gases

FuseSchool

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KRLNDTmBFZY)

### Understand it

Gas particles move in random directions and collide with other particles. Overall, a gas spreads from where its particles are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. This is diffusion, not a plan made by the particles. After mixing, particles keep moving even when there is no overall concentration difference. Use the flower-smell example as a particle model; do not sniff unknown or rotting materials. Real smells can also be carried by air currents.

### Try it yourself

Two gases have mixed evenly inside a closed container. Have their particles stopped moving? Explain what has changed and what has not.

**Check:** No. Random motion continues. The gases are now evenly mixed, so there is no overall movement from a more concentrated region to a less concentrated one.

### Extend your reasoning

Gas particles move in random directions and collide. If more particles of a gas are concentrated in one region than another, their random motion can produce a net spread toward the less concentrated region. That overall pattern is diffusion; individual particles do not choose a destination or all move in the same direction.

Evenly mixed does not mean motionless: particles keep moving. Imagine counting crossings through an imaginary boundary after mixing: particles can cross both ways, with no persistent overall transfer in one direction. Equal concentrations describe the distribution, not the absence of motion. Real smells may also travel with air currents, so smell spreading alone does not isolate diffusion. Do not sniff unknown or rotting materials.

### Try an additional case

In a model of an evenly mixed gas, 12 particles cross an imaginary boundary left to right during one interval and 12 cross right to left. Find the net transfer. Does that result mean no particle moved? Would one short interval with 13 crossings one way and 11 the other prove the gas has permanently stopped being well mixed?

**Reasoning:** The net transfer is 12 - 12 = 0, although 24 crossing events occurred. Zero net transfer is not zero motion. Small random differences can occur over a short interval; 13 - 11 = 2 in that interval alone does not prove a lasting concentration difference. Look for the overall pattern, not a plan made by individual particles.

## 4. Changes of state

### Watch: Changes of State

FuseSchool

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xYU7RSoOZ0U)

### Understand it

Follow melting, freezing, evaporation and condensation, then draw particles before and after one change. Heating or cooling changes particle motion and arrangement. During ice melting, water molecules remain water molecules: the attractions between molecules change, not the bonds holding each water molecule together. In a closed system that loses no matter, total mass stays the same. Water left in an open dish can leave as vapour, so the dish's mass can fall without matter being destroyed. Watch the demonstrations; do not handle dry ice or boiling water yourself.

### Try it yourself

Ice melts inside a sealed container. Which should change: the arrangement of the water particles, the kind of molecule, or the total mass of the container and contents?

**Check:** The arrangement changes. The molecules remain water, and the total mass stays the same because no matter enters or leaves.

### Extend your reasoning

Melting, freezing, evaporation and condensation change the arrangement and motion of particles. When ice melts, water molecules remain water molecules; the attractions between molecules change, not the bonds holding each water molecule together. Attractions between molecules and their arrangement change. Do not use the same spacing rule for every solid-liquid pair: ice occupies more volume than the same mass of liquid water.

State what is inside the measured system. For a closed system such as a sealed container with no matter entering or leaving, total mass of container and contents stays the same during a state change. In an open dish, water vapour can leave the measured dish, so its mass can fall without matter being destroyed. These are reasoning models: watch the demonstrations, and do not heat sealed containers. Do not handle dry ice or boiling water yourself.

### Try an additional case

A closed container and its ice have a total mass of 180 g. All the ice melts without anything entering or leaving. What is the final total mass, and have the water molecules become a new substance? In a separate open-dish example, the dish and water go from 180 g to 174 g as water evaporates. Account for the 6 g difference.

**Reasoning:** The closed container and contents still total 180 g, and the molecules remain water. In the open example, 6 g of water has left the measured dish as vapour, under the stated evaporation-only model. It has not vanished from existence. The different measured system boundaries explain the different mass results.
