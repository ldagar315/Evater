## 1. Mean as fair share or balance

### Watch: Mean | Data sets and distribution | 6th grade math (Illustrative Math-aligned) | Khan Academy

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_40su2dEvA4)

### Understand it

The mean is the amount each entry would have after sharing the total equally. For 4, 6, 8 and 10, the total is 28 and the mean is 7. The mean need not appear among the original values. You can also see it as a balance: the total shortfall below 7 equals the total excess above 7.

Work backwards using total = mean × number of entries. To include one more entry, update both the total and the number of entries; do not simply average the old mean and the new value, since the old mean stands for several entries. Adding a value above the old mean raises the mean.

### Try it yourself

Five reading sessions have a mean length of 12 minutes. Four lengths are 8, 10, 14 and 18 minutes. Find the missing fifth length. A sixth session lasts 18 minutes. Find the new mean. A learner averages 12 and 18 to get 15 minutes. Explain why that is not the six-session mean.

**Check:** The five-session total is 5 × 12 = 60 minutes. The four listed lengths total 50, so the missing length is 10 minutes. The new total is 60 + 18 = 78 across six sessions, giving 78/6 = 13 minutes. Averaging 12 and 18 gives the old five-session group and the single new session equal weight, which is not equal weight per session.

## 2. Reading graphs and axes

### Watch: Misleading line graphs

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gs-OPF3KEGU)

### Understand it

Read a graph's title, axis labels, units and scale before comparing heights. If each grid step is 5 units, three steps mean 15 units. To find a point's reading, also include the starting value shown on that axis. The bottom of the visible plot is not always zero.

Two charts can show the same numerical change with different-looking heights because their scales or starting values differ. Translate the picture back into numbers before comparing changes. A cut axis is a reason to read carefully, not proof by itself that the plotted data is false.

### Try it yourself

Chart A's vertical scale starts at 80 units and increases by 2 per grid step. Two marked readings are 5 and 8 steps above that start. Chart B starts at zero and increases by 10 units per step; its corresponding readings are 9 and 9.6 steps above zero. Find both pairs of readings and their changes. Which chart shows the larger numerical increase?

**Check:** A shows 80 + 5 × 2 = 90 and 80 + 8 × 2 = 96, an increase of 6 units. B shows 9 × 10 = 90 and 9.6 × 10 = 96, also an increase of 6 units. Neither increase is larger. The same data can occupy different numbers of grid steps, so compare labelled values rather than apparent height alone.

## 3. Line graphs and change over time

### Watch: Reading line graphs

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=36v2EXZRzUE)

### Understand it

A line graph shows how a quantity changes along the horizontal axis, often time. Read the units on both axes and subtract labelled values. The video uses monthly stock prices, but the same reading method works for other quantities. Connecting recorded points with straight lines is a display choice; it does not reveal every unrecorded value.

To compare rates of change, divide the vertical change by the elapsed time. Equal rises over unequal time intervals are not equal rates. A flat segment means the vertical-axis reading is constant, not automatically zero; its meaning depends on the quantity being measured.

### Try it yourself

A tank's recorded volumes are 20 L at 0 minutes, 50 L at 5 minutes, 50 L at 8 minutes and 80 L at 10 minutes. Find the average filling rate over 0-5 minutes and 8-10 minutes. What volume does a straight segment joining the 5- and 8-minute readings represent? Do those two readings prove the volume never varied between them, or that every possible inflow and outflow was zero?

**Check:** From 0 to 5 minutes the rate is (50 - 20)/5 = 6 L/min. From 8 to 10 minutes it is (80 - 50)/2 = 15 L/min, so the later interval is faster despite the same 30 L rise. The plotted flat segment represents 50 L, not an empty tank. The two equal readings do not rule out unrecorded changes between them. Even a truly constant volume would not prove no flows: equal inflow and outflow could balance.

## 4. Choosing and interpreting a display

### Watch: Misleading line graphs

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gs-OPF3KEGU)

### Understand it

Choose a display for the question. A line graph can show a quantity changing over time. Comparing groups at a single time may be clearer with bars, while a table gives exact values. Check what was measured and what is missing before drawing a conclusion.

If two quantities rise together, the graph shows an association, not proof that one caused the other. A third factor or a difference in how data was collected may help explain the pattern. Describe what the observations support first, then suggest an explanation that could be checked instead of treating the explanation as a fact.

### Try it yourself

In a made-up dataset, a park records more ice-cream sales and more sunhat wearers on hotter days. A learner claims selling ice cream makes people wear hats. What association is recorded, and what other factor could help explain both? Would drawing the same observations with bars instead of lines establish the claimed cause?

**Check:** The recorded association is that sales and hat counts are both higher on hotter days. Hotter weather is a plausible common factor, so these observations alone do not show that ice-cream sales cause hat wearing. Redrawing the same data changes its display, not the evidence for a cause. Ask what other variables or comparisons would test the explanation.
