## 1. Angle sums in quadrilaterals

### Watch: Angles in a Quadrilateral

corbettmaths

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y9udtA3cPgU)

### Understand it

The interior angles of a simple quadrilateral total 360°. For a convex quadrilateral, either diagonal divides the region into two triangles. Adding their angle sums gives 180° + 180° = 360°; the two pieces of each split corner add back to the original corner angle. This is a reason, not an estimate from a drawing.

If three angles are 65°, 85° and 110°, their sum is 260°, leaving 100°. If angles are described by expressions, add those expressions first and set the total equal to 360°. Keep the distinction between an angle, such as 2x degrees, and the value of x. Do not apply this argument to a self-crossing bow-tie drawing as though it enclosed the same simple region.

### Try it yourself

The interior angles of a convex quadrilateral are 2x°, 3x°, 4x° and 6x°. Find x and all four angles, then check their total. Why is reporting just x not a complete answer? Would rotating the drawing change the calculation?

**Check:** The sum is 15x = 360, so x = 24. The four angles are 48°, 72°, 96° and 144°, which total 360°. The value of x is a shared parameter, not the list of actual angles. Rotation changes neither angle measures nor the diagonal argument.

## 2. Parallelograms and diagonals

### Watch: Why parallelogram diagonals bisect each other

Kevinmathscience

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PC9mbAwE3zk)

Follow the two opposite triangles in the proof. The video uses two angles and a corresponding non-included side, often called AAS or SAA. It proves that each diagonal is cut into two equal halves, not that the two whole diagonals are equal or perpendicular. It does not prove the converse. Then return to the original explanation and question below.

### Understand it

Let the diagonals AC and BD of parallelogram ABCD meet at O. Opposite sides AB and CD are equal, and parallel sides give equal alternate angles. So triangles AOB and COD are congruent by ASA. Corresponding sides give AO = OC and BO = OD: the diagonals bisect each other.

If AC = 18 cm and BD = 12 cm, then AO = OC = 9 cm and BO = OD = 6 cm. Do not set AO equal to BO: bisecting each other does not make the two diagonals equal. For a converse check, draw two segments crossing at their midpoints and join their endpoints in order. Equal halves and vertically opposite angles give congruent triangles, helping explain why the result is a parallelogram.

### Try it yourself

In parallelogram PQRS, the diagonals meet at M. PM = 3x - 1 cm, MR = x + 7 cm, and QM = 5 cm. Find x, PR and QS. Must PR = QS?

**Check:** PM = MR, so 3x - 1 = x + 7 and x = 4. Each half of PR is 11 cm, giving PR = 22 cm. QS = 10 cm. The unequal diagonals are allowed.

## 3. Rectangles, rhombuses and squares

### Watch: Quadrilateral properties | Perimeter, area, and volume | Geometry | Khan Academy

Khan Academy

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5CeBlu260Rw)

### Understand it

A rectangle has four right angles; a rhombus has four equal sides. A square meets both conditions, so it is both a rectangle and a rhombus. A rectangle with adjacent sides 10 cm and 16 cm is not a square: having right angles does not force all sides to match. A rhombus with one angle 70° is not a rectangle: equal sides do not force right angles.

Classify from the stated properties, not whether a shape looks upright or tilted. These categories overlap, so a request to name every applicable category may have more than one correct label. Calling a square a rectangle is correct but does not list all its properties.

### Try it yourself

Classify each shape as rectangle, rhombus and/or square: A has four right angles and adjacent sides 6 cm and 10 cm; B has four equal sides of 9 cm and one angle of 60°; C has four equal sides of 7 cm and four right angles. Give every applicable label. Does turning C on the page remove any label?

**Check:** A is a rectangle, not a rhombus or square, because its adjacent side lengths differ. B is a rhombus, not a rectangle or square, because a 60° angle is not a right angle. C is a square and therefore also a rectangle and a rhombus. Turning C preserves lengths and angles, so all three labels remain correct.

## 4. Kites, trapeziums and constructions

### Watch: Construct a kite with a compass and straightedge

JoAnn's School

[Open the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-IRXWg2oLY4)

Use the perpendicular bisector to locate vertices equally far from the two endpoints. This video covers the kite construction, not trapezium angles.

### Understand it

For a kite ABCD with AB = AD and CB = CD, points A and C are each equally distant from B and D. The line AC is the perpendicular bisector of BD. This does not mean BD also bisects AC. To construct such a kite, draw BD, then use two different compass radii from B and D to locate A and C on opposite sides. Keep each radius greater than half BD so the arcs meet off the line; using the same radius for A and C produces a rhombus instead of showing a general kite.

Here, a trapezium has **at least one pair of parallel opposite sides**, so parallelograms are included. Draw AB, choose D away from it, and draw a line through D parallel to AB. Choose C on that line so ABCD is a simple quadrilateral. If AB is parallel to CD and angle A is 68°, angle D is 180° - 68° = 112°, using the angles on the same side of transversal AD.

### Try it yourself

A trapezium PQRS has PQ parallel to SR. Angle P is 74° and angle Q is 101°. Find angles S and R. Must the trapezium be isosceles?

**Check:** S = 106° and R = 79°. Each pair on a non-parallel side adds to 180°, and all four total 360°. It is not isosceles: the two angles on base PQ are unequal.
