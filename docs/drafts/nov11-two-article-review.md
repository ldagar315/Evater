# NCERT Class 8 Maths Chapter 4: Quadrilaterals - Study Guide

Chapter 4 of Ganita Prakash takes the four-sided shapes you have drawn since primary school and asks a harder question: what exactly makes a square a square, and when is a rhombus secretly a square too?

### What this chapter is about

Ganita Prakash Chapter 4 is about four-sided figures - quadrilaterals. You start with the shapes you already know, rectangles and squares, then widen the family: parallelograms, rhombuses, kites and trapeziums. The point is not memorising a list. The chapter keeps asking you to fold, measure and argue until each property feels discovered rather than dictated.

### The angle sum that starts everything

Split any quadrilateral along a diagonal and you get two triangles. Each triangle's angles add up to 180 degrees, so the four angles of any quadrilateral add up to 360 degrees, whatever its shape. This one fact powers most of the chapter's reasoning. If three angles of a quadrilateral are 80, 100 and 70 degrees, the fourth has to be 110, because the total must be 360.

### The six shapes and how they relate

- Rectangle: four right angles. Opposite sides equal, diagonals equal in length and bisecting each other.
- Square: a rectangle with all four sides equal. Diagonals equal, bisecting each other, and meeting at 90 degrees.
- Parallelogram: both pairs of opposite sides parallel. Opposite sides and opposite angles equal, diagonals bisecting each other.
- Rhombus: all four sides equal. Opposite angles equal, diagonals bisecting each other at 90 degrees and bisecting the corner angles.
- Kite: two non-overlapping adjacent pairs of equal sides (AB = BC and CD = DA). Every rhombus is also a kite.
- Trapezium: at least one pair of parallel opposite sides - so in this book, every parallelogram is also a trapezium.

### The family insight: a square is a rectangle and a rhombus

This is the idea students trip over. A special case is not a different shape. A square has four equal sides, so it satisfies the rhombus definition. It also has four right angles, so it satisfies the rectangle definition. A square is both: a rhombus with right angles, or a rectangle with equal sides. The reverse is false - a rhombus need not be a square (its angles can be slanted) and a rectangle need not be a square (its sides can be unequal). Rectangles, squares and rhombuses are all special parallelograms, because in each one both pairs of opposite sides are parallel. The kite family overlaps here too: every rhombus is a kite, and the one shape that is both a kite and a rectangle is the square. The chapter's own exercise asks you to draw this family map.

### Common mistakes to avoid

- Using 180 degrees for a quadrilateral's angle sum. That is the triangle rule; quadrilaterals total 360 degrees.
- Saying "a square is not a rhombus". It is - a rhombus with right angles.
- Thinking four equal sides make a square. A rhombus has four equal sides and need have no right angle.
- Assuming diagonals always bisect each other. That holds for parallelograms (including rectangles, rhombuses and squares) but not for kites or for trapeziums that are not parallelograms.

### How to practise this chapter

Sketch each shape from its definition alone, then check which properties your sketch actually shows. Play the takeaway game: a rhombus, but one angle is exactly 90 degrees - what shape do you get? (A square.) Short mixed question sets turn these definitions into reflex. Evater practice tests can help you turn chapter reading into an attempt-and-feedback loop, free to try.

# NCERT Class 8 Science Chapter 6: Pressure, Winds, Storms, and Cyclones - Study Guide

Why do water tanks sit on the roof, why can a storm rip a roof off a house, and what makes a cyclone spin? Curiosity Chapter 6 answers all three with one idea: pressure.

### What this chapter is about

The new Class 8 Science book, Curiosity, devotes Chapter 6 to a chain of ideas that runs from a schoolbag strap to a tropical cyclone. Force acting over an area gives pressure. Air itself exerts pressure. Differences in air pressure make wind. And extreme pressure differences build storms, thunderstorms and cyclones. Learn the chain in order and each event stops being a separate fact to memorise.

### Pressure: force acting on an area

The same force feels sharper over a smaller area. That is why a nail's point enters wood while its flat head does not, and why wide bag straps hurt your shoulders less than thin ones. Pressure is force divided by area, measured in pascals (N/m2). Liquids exert pressure too, and it grows with depth - which is why water tanks are placed at a height: the tall column of water below the tank supplies the pressure that pushes water through your taps.

### Air pushes too

Air has weight, and the whole atmosphere presses on us from every direction. We do not feel it because our bodies push back with equal pressure. You can see it with a rubber sucker pressed onto a smooth tile: pressing pushes most of the air out, and the outside air pressure holds the sucker in place.

### Pressure differences make wind

Uneven heating of the Earth's surface creates pressure differences, and air moves from a high-pressure region to a low-pressure one. That moving air is wind, and the bigger the pressure difference, the faster it blows. Sea breeze and land breeze are the everyday examples: by day the land heats faster, warm air rises, and cooler air flows in from the sea; at night the pattern reverses. The chapter shows one more twist with two hanging balloons: blow air between them and they move together, because fast-moving air has lower pressure. The same effect can lift roofs in a storm - the wind above the roof lowers the pressure there, and the higher pressure inside pushes the roof up. That is why keeping doors and windows open during a storm can save a roof.

### Storms, lightning and cyclones

When warm, moist air rises fast, it cools, its water vapour condenses into clouds, and rain, hail or snow follows: a storm. Inside storm clouds, ice particles and water droplets rub together and separate electric charge. When the built-up charge jumps - within a cloud, between clouds, or down to the ground - you see lightning and hear thunder. Pre-monsoon thunderstorms have local names, like Kalboishakhi in West Bengal, Bihar and Jharkhand and Bordoisila in Assam. A pointed metal rod running from above the roof deep into the ground, a lightning conductor, carries the charge safely away.

A cyclone is what happens when this process feeds itself over a warm ocean. Rising moist air condenses and releases heat, which warms the air further, which makes it rise faster, creating ever-lower pressure. Surrounding air rushes in, and the Earth's rotation sets the whole system spinning. The calm centre of lowest pressure is the eye; the strongest winds and heaviest rain ring it. Cyclone Amphan in 2020 reached wind speeds of 270 km/h. Once a cyclone crosses land it loses its supply of moist air and weakens, but its winds, rain and storm surge - seawater pushed as high as 3 to 12 metres - can devastate coastal areas. The India Meteorological Department (IMD) tracks cyclones and issues warnings, so knowing the science also means taking those alerts seriously.

### Common mistakes to avoid

- Mixing up force and pressure. A small force on a tiny area can produce more pressure than a large force spread wide.
- Saying wind blows from low pressure to high pressure. It is the other way: high to low, like air escaping a balloon.
- Thinking the eye of the cyclone is the most dangerous part. The eye is calm; the ring around it carries the strongest winds.
- Thinking lightning comes from clouds colliding. It is electric charge, built up by rubbing ice and water droplets inside the cloud, suddenly discharging.

### How to practise this chapter

Explain each link of the chain aloud - force and area, air pressure, wind, storm, cyclone - then test yourself with mixed questions until the order is automatic. Notice that Chapter 5's ideas are doing the work here: pressure is force acting on an area. Evater practice tests can help you turn chapter reading into an attempt-and-feedback loop, free to try.
